# Start here: you do not have to do it all today

When someone close to you dies, the world asks a great deal of you very quickly. There are calls to make, forms to sign, people to tell, and a stream of paperwork that seems to arrive while you are still in shock.

Here is the most important thing in this book: very little of it has to happen today.

In the first day or two, only a few things are truly urgent: making sure the death is officially recorded, choosing a funeral home, and keeping the person's home, pets and belongings safe. Almost everything else can wait a week, and much of it can wait a month or more. Lists that put forty tasks on the first day make grief harder, not easier.

This book takes the work in the order it usually needs to happen:

- the first day or two;
- the first week;
- the first month;
- finding every account and piece of property;
- bills and debts;
- closing the estate.

At the back you will find a checklist of papers to find, and fill-in pages for tasks, accounts, calls and money.

## Papers, not advice

This book helps you find, keep and track papers. It does not give legal or tax advice. Whether an estate needs to go through probate, who inherits what, which debts are paid and in what order, and what taxes apply all depend on the state, the size and kind of the estate, and whether there is a will. The probate court's own self-help pages, a probate lawyer or a tax preparer can tell you what applies. The official sources at the back of this book list probate court pages for your state, each with the date it was checked.

## Who this book is for

You might be the named executor in a will. You might be the closest relative of someone who left no will. You might be helping a parent who has lost a spouse. You might simply be the one in the family who is good with paperwork. Whatever your role, start here.

# The first day or two

## Make sure the death is officially recorded

- **If the person died in a hospital, nursing home or under hospice care,** the staff will usually handle the official pronouncement of death and help with the next steps.
- **If the person died at home and was not under hospice care,** call 911. Emergency services will come, and the death will be pronounced.
- **If the person died at home under hospice care,** call the hospice. They will tell you what to do.

## Choose a funeral home

The funeral home will collect the person, help you plan a funeral, burial or cremation, and usually file the death certificate with the state. If the person left written wishes or a prepaid funeral plan, look for it now. Ask the funeral home for a written list of prices before you agree to anything; funeral homes are required to provide one.

## Order death certificates

The funeral home can usually order certified copies of the death certificate for you. You will need them to close accounts, claim benefits, transfer property and deal with the court. Many families find they need more than they expected, because many offices want their own certified copy. Ask the funeral home how many people in your situation usually order, and know that you can order more later from the state or county vital records office.

## Keep things safe

- Lock the person's home and car. Take any spare keys.
- Make sure pets are looked after.
- Collect valuables, cash, checkbooks and important papers you can see, and keep them somewhere secure.
- If the home will be empty, ask a neighbor to watch it, and keep lights on a timer.
- Do not give away or sell belongings yet, even if family members ask. Until it is clear who has the legal right to deal with them, it is safer to wait.

## Tell the closest people

Call or visit the people who should hear it personally. Ask one or two relatives or friends to share the news with everyone else, so you do not have to repeat it many times.

## Look after yourself

Eat something. Drink water. Sleep if you can. Accept help when it is offered: food, childcare, someone to sit with you while you make calls.

# The first week

## Find the will and any letter of wishes

Look in the person's home papers, a safe, a filing cabinet, or with their lawyer. A will names an executor, sometimes called a personal representative, and says who should inherit. Some people also leave a separate letter about funeral wishes, passwords or personal items. Do not mark or unstaple an original will. Keep it safe; the probate court may need the original.

If you cannot find a will, ask the person's lawyer, check whether the local probate court holds wills on deposit in your state, and look for any safe deposit box key.

## Arrange the funeral or memorial

Work with the funeral home and family. It is fine to keep it simple. If money is tight, ask the funeral home about lower-cost options, and ask the person's employer, union, faith community or veterans office whether any help with funeral costs is available.

## Tell Social Security

In many cases the funeral home reports the death to Social Security. Ask them whether they will. If the person received Social Security or other government benefits, those payments usually stop, and any payment for the month of death or after may have to be returned. Do not spend money paid into the person's account after death until you know whether it must be returned. A surviving spouse or children may be entitled to benefits; ask Social Security.

## Tell the employer, if they were working

The employer can tell you about final pay, life insurance through work, retirement accounts and any other benefits.

## Forward the mail

The mail is one of the most useful ways to find accounts and bills. Ask the post office about forwarding the person's mail to you. Keep every envelope that arrives for the next several months.

## Start your logs

Start the task list and the calls and letters log at the back of this book now. Each time you call an office, note the date, the office, any reference number, what they said and what happens next. You will make many calls, and they blur together quickly.

# The first month

## Decide whether probate is needed

Probate is the court process that confirms who has the authority to deal with the estate, pays debts and passes property to the people who inherit. Whether it is needed depends on the state, what the person owned, and how it was owned.

Some property passes outside probate, for example:

- accounts with a named beneficiary, such as life insurance, retirement accounts and payable-on-death bank accounts;
- property owned jointly with someone who survives them, in many cases;
- property held in a trust.

Many states also have simpler procedures for small estates, sometimes using a sworn statement called a small estate affidavit instead of full probate. The rules and limits differ by state. The official sources at the back of this book list your state's probate and small estate pages, each with the date it was checked. The probate court's self-help center or a probate lawyer can tell you which route fits.

## Getting authority to act

If probate is needed, the usual result is a paper from the court that gives the executor or administrator authority to act for the estate. Banks and other companies will ask to see it. Keep several copies, and log who you have sent it to.

## Life insurance and benefits

Look for life insurance policies, pension papers, annuities and benefit statements. Contact each company. They will ask for a certified death certificate and a claim form. A named beneficiary usually claims directly.

## Protect against identity theft

Sadly, identity thieves target people who have died. Ask the three major credit bureaus to note the death on the person's credit file. Cancel the person's driver's license or state ID through the state motor vehicle office, and close credit cards once you have a list of them.

## Open an estate bank account, if needed

If the estate will receive and pay money, an executor often opens a separate bank account in the name of the estate. The bank will tell you what it needs. Do not mix estate money with your own. Log every payment in and out in the money log at the back of this book.

# Finding every account and property

The hardest part of settling an estate is often simply finding everything. People keep accounts in many places, and few leave a complete list.

## Where to look

- **The mail and email.** Statements, renewal notices and tax forms arrive over the following months.
- **The person's papers.** Filing cabinets, drawers, safes, folders, address books.
- **Their phone and computer**, if you have lawful access. Banking apps, emails from companies, saved passwords.
- **Last year's tax return.** It lists interest, dividends, retirement distributions and other income, which point to accounts.
- **Checkbook registers and bank statements.** Regular payments point to insurance, loans, subscriptions and storage units.
- **Their employer and former employers**, for retirement accounts and life insurance.
- **Their state's unclaimed property office.** Every state keeps a free search of money held for people who lost track of it.

## Make the list

Use the accounts and property log at the back of this book. For each account or property, write what it is, where the papers are, who to contact and its status. Add to it every time a new envelope arrives.

## Real estate and family land

Find the deed for any home or land the person owned. The county recorder or register of deeds usually keeps copies of recorded deeds. If the property was passed down in the family without a will, it may now be owned by many relatives together, sometimes called heirs' property. Papers that show who owned it, how it passed, and who has paid the property taxes can matter a great deal for families in this situation. Keep tax bills and any old deeds, wills or family records you find, and ask a lawyer or legal aid office that works on heirs' property before anyone signs anything about the land.

## Digital accounts

Email, social media, photo storage, online banking and subscriptions all need attention. Many companies have a process for the family or executor to close or memorialise an account. Keep a list of digital accounts and what you did with each one. Do not log in as the person to make transactions unless a lawyer says it is allowed.

## Vehicles

Find the title for each car. The state motor vehicle office can explain how to transfer or sell a vehicle after a death. Keep the car insured until it is transferred or sold.

# Bills, debts and what not to pay yet

Bills will keep arriving. Some are true debts of the estate. Some are for services that should simply be cancelled. Some are scams.

## Log every bill

Use the calls and letters log. For each bill or letter: who it is from, the date received, what it asks, and the date you answered.

## Do not pay from your own pocket

In general, family members are not personally responsible for a relative's debts just because they are family, though there are exceptions, for example a debt you co-signed, a joint account, or rules in some states about spouses. Debt collectors sometimes call relatives and imply otherwise. Ask a lawyer or the probate court's self-help center before you pay any of the person's debts with your own money.

## Ask before paying estate debts

When an estate does pay debts, many states set an order in which debts are paid, and an executor who pays the wrong bills first can run into trouble. Ask the probate court's self-help center or a lawyer before paying debts out of estate money, other than ordinary costs of looking after property, such as keeping the lights and insurance on.

## Cancel what is no longer needed

Phone plans, streaming services, gym memberships, magazine subscriptions, newspaper delivery. Call or write to cancel, give the date of death, and ask for a written confirmation. Log each one.

## Keep the house running

Utilities, insurance and property taxes on a home usually need to continue while the property is being dealt with. Tell the insurance company that the owner has died and the home may be empty; some policies change when a home is unoccupied.

## Watch for scams

Be wary of anyone who calls demanding immediate payment, threatens arrest, or asks for gift cards or wire transfers. Real companies send letters and can wait for proper paperwork. If unsure, hang up and call the company on a number from its official website or a past statement.

# Closing the estate

Settling an estate can take months, sometimes longer. The final stages usually look something like this, though the steps and their names differ by state.

## The inventory

Many courts ask the executor to list what the estate owns and its value. Your accounts and property log is the starting point.

## Taxes

The person may need a final income tax return for the year they died, and the estate itself may need its own tax return if it earns income. A tax preparer can tell you what is needed. Keep the tax forms that arrive in the mail for the person and for the estate.

## Paying debts and expenses

With the court's process or a lawyer's guidance, the executor pays valid debts and expenses of the estate, keeping receipts and logging each payment.

## Passing property to the people who inherit

When debts and taxes are dealt with, property is passed to the people who inherit under the will, or under state law if there was no will. Get a signed receipt for each distribution. Some courts require a final accounting showing every dollar in and out; the money log at the back of this book makes that much easier.

## Keep the records

Keep the estate's papers, your logs and the final court papers for several years after the estate is closed. A tax preparer or lawyer can tell you how long.

# Looking after yourself

Settling an estate means doing careful paperwork while grieving. It is normal to feel tired, forgetful, angry or numb, sometimes all in one day.

- **Share the work.** One person can handle the mail, another the house, another the calls. Keep one shared task list so nothing is done twice.
- **Set short sessions.** An hour of paperwork, then a break.
- **Write everything down.** Grief affects memory. Your logs remember for you.
- **Accept help.** Friends who ask "what can I do?" often mean it. Give them a specific task.
- **Find support.** Hospices often offer bereavement support to families, sometimes even if the person was not in their care. Faith communities, grief groups and counselors help too.
- **Expect family tension.** Money and belongings can strain relationships. Keeping clear records and sharing information openly helps. If it becomes serious, a mediator or lawyer can help.

You are doing a hard thing. One step at a time is enough.
