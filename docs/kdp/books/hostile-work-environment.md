# What the phrase means, and what this book can do

People say "hostile work environment" to describe a job that has become hard to bear: a manager who screams, coworkers who mock, jokes that target who you are, being frozen out, threats, a boss who changes your hours after you complain. In everyday speech the phrase covers all of that.

In law, the phrase is narrower. Under federal and state employment laws, a workplace is usually called legally hostile only when the harassment is tied to a protected characteristic, such as race, color, religion, sex (including pregnancy, sexual orientation and gender identity), national origin, age, disability or genetic information, or to something like a complaint you made about discrimination, and when it is severe or frequent enough to change the conditions of your job. Many state and local laws add more protected groups. A boss who is rude to everyone may be miserable to work for without being illegal.

This book does not decide which kind of situation you are in. Nobody can do that from a book. What this book does is help you keep a clear, calm, dated record of what has happened, so that the people who can advise you, such as your company's human resources team, a government agency, a union representative or a lawyer, can understand it quickly.

## Why a record matters

Workplace problems tend to arrive as a long series of small moments. Each one on its own sounds minor when you try to explain it later. Months afterwards, you remember that it was bad, but not exactly what was said or on which day.

A record made at the time fixes that. It turns a feeling into a list of dated events that someone else can follow. It also helps you see patterns: whether things got worse after a complaint, whether your hours changed, whether the same person is involved each time.

A record you keep is your own account. It is not proof of anything on its own, and nothing here can predict how an employer, an agency or a court sees it. But it is the raw material every next step needs.

## The scope rule

Keep only what you received, what was sent to you, and what you wrote yourself.

- Do not copy company files, customer data, other employees' records or anything marked confidential, even if you have access to it at work. Taking material you are not allowed to take can break company policy or the law and can give your employer a reason to act against you.
- Do not forward work emails to your personal account if your company's policy forbids it. Ask a lawyer how to keep copies safely.
- Do not record conversations in secret. Rules on recording differ by state, and in some states recording without everyone's consent is a crime.
- Do not look through anyone else's desk, computer, phone or accounts.

When in doubt, write it down instead. Your own dated notes are always yours.

## Deadlines exist

Complaints to government agencies about discrimination and harassment usually have deadlines, and they can be short. The time limit depends on the law involved and the state you work in. The official sources at the back of this book list the agencies and deadlines for your state, each with the date it was checked. If you think you might want to make a complaint, find out the deadline early, even before you decide anything else.

# Incident notes that stay useful

The single most useful habit is a short note written the same day as each incident.

## What every note includes

- **Date and time.** As exact as you can.
- **Place.** The office, the break room, a video call, a text message.
- **Who was there.** The person involved and anyone who saw or heard it.
- **What was said or done.** Exact words in quotation marks when you remember them. Describe actions plainly.
- **What you did after.** Walked away, told a supervisor, went home sick, said nothing.

## Facts, not conclusions

Write what a camera would have recorded. Leave out what you think the person meant, what kind of person they are, and what you think it means legally.

An invented weak note:

> My manager harassed me again today because he's sexist and he hates me. He's trying to get me fired. This is a hostile work environment.

An invented useful note about the same day:

> Monday, around ten in the morning, team meeting room. Present: my manager, two coworkers on my team. When I presented the sales numbers, my manager said, "Maybe leave the math to the guys next time." One coworker laughed. I finished the presentation. Afterwards I told my coworker at the next desk that the comment upset me. I wrote this at lunch.

The second note lets a reader see exactly what happened and decide for themselves what it means. It also names who was present, which matters if anyone is asked about it later.

## Keep notes somewhere that is yours

Use this book, a personal notebook, the free printable log, or an app on your own phone. Do not keep your only notes on a work computer or in a work account; you may lose access without warning.

## Corrections

If you get something wrong, do not erase it. Add a dated line underneath: "Correction: the meeting was Tuesday, not Monday." A record that shows its corrections openly is easier to trust than one that has been rewritten.

## Things that are not one moment

Some problems are not single incidents: being left off meetings, assignments shifting to others, being moved to a worse desk or shift. Note these too, with the date you noticed them and what changed.

# Saving messages and papers you are allowed to keep

Messages and papers sent to you are part of your record. The trick is keeping copies without breaking a rule.

## Usually yours to keep

- Your offer letter, job description and any contract.
- Your own pay stubs and tax forms.
- Your performance reviews and any warnings given to you.
- The employee handbook and policies you were given.
- Text messages sent to your personal phone.
- Letters mailed to your home.
- Notes you wrote yourself.

## Ask before you copy

- Work emails sent to you. Many companies forbid forwarding them to personal accounts. A lawyer can advise you on how to preserve them safely, and in some cases can ask the company to keep them.
- Messages in work chat tools.
- Anything containing customer, patient or other employees' information. Leave it alone.

## How to keep copies

- Take screenshots that show the date, time and sender, and capture the whole conversation around a message, not one line out of context.
- Keep the originals on your phone as well. Do not delete threads.
- Keep a backup in a second place you control.
- Never edit, crop or annotate a copy in a way that changes it. If you want to add a comment, write it separately in your log.

## Name the copies so you can find them

A simple file name helps later: the date first, then a few words. For example: "Monday team meeting, manager comment, text from coworker". Keep a short index in your log of what you saved and where.

# Pay, hours and schedule changes

Sometimes a hostile situation shows up in your pay: shifts cut, overtime unpaid, a final paycheck late, hours changed after you complained. A simple habit makes these easy to see.

## Keep your own hours

Write down when you start and stop each day, including breaks, on your own paper or phone. At the end of each pay period, compare your total with the hours on your pay stub.

## Compare with the stub

The hours and pay log at the back gives you one line per pay period: the hours you worked, the hours on the stub, the amount paid, and where the stub is kept. A difference is not automatically a violation; there may be a simple explanation. But a written list of differences is much easier to ask about than a vague sense that you have been underpaid.

## Schedule changes

If your schedule, duties or hours change, note the date you were told, who told you, what changed, and any reason given. If the change came soon after a complaint, write down both dates.

## Minimum wage, overtime and final pay

Rules on minimum wage, overtime and how quickly a final paycheck must be paid differ by state. The official sources at the back of this book list your state's labor department pages for these, each with the date it was checked. Your state labor department can answer questions about pay at no cost.

# Putting a complaint in writing

Many people complain out loud, in a hallway, and then have nothing to show for it. A short written complaint is clearer for everyone.

## Find the process first

Look in your employee handbook for the harassment or complaint policy. It usually names who to report to: a manager, human resources, an ethics line, or someone other than your manager if your manager is involved. Following the company's own process matters.

## Keep it short and factual

A written complaint does not need legal words. It needs the facts.

An invented example:

> To: Human Resources
> Subject: Complaint about comments at work
>
> I am writing to report comments made to me by my manager. On Monday during the team meeting he said, "Maybe leave the math to the guys next time." On Wednesday by the printer he said, "Smile more, sweetheart, it's not that hard." Two coworkers were present at the meeting. These comments have made it hard for me to do my job. I would like to discuss this and understand what happens next.
>
> Thank you.

Keep a copy of what you send, with the date. If you report in a meeting, send a short email afterwards that sums up what you said and what you were told: "Thank you for meeting with me today. To confirm, I reported the comments on Monday and Wednesday, and you said you would look into it and update me."

## Keep every reply

Log each complaint and each reply in the complaints log: the date, who you told, how, what you reported, and what they said back, with its date.

## If things change after you complain

Laws in many places protect employees from being punished for making a good-faith complaint about discrimination or harassment. If your hours, duties, reviews or treatment change after a complaint, write down each change with its date, next to the date of the complaint. Then ask an agency or a lawyer about it.

# Turning your notes into a one-page summary

When you meet someone who can help, they will not have time to read every note. A one-page summary in date order lets them see the whole story in a few minutes, and points them to the notes behind it.

## How to build it

- Go through your incident log, your complaints log and your pay log.
- Pick the events that matter most: the first incident, the most serious ones, each complaint, each reply, and any change in your job after a complaint.
- Write one line for each, in date order: the date, then a few plain words.
- Keep it to one page. Everything else stays in the logs, ready if they ask.

## An invented example

> Summary of events, prepared by me for a first meeting.
>
> Early March. New manager joins the team.
> Mid March. Comment in team meeting about leaving the math "to the guys". Two coworkers present. Note made same day.
> Late March. Comment by the printer: "Smile more, sweetheart." No one else present. Note made same day.
> Early April. Left off the invitation to the client planning meeting for the first time. Note made.
> Early April. Written complaint to human resources by email. Copy kept.
> Mid April. Meeting with human resources. Follow-up email sent the same afternoon summing it up.
> Late April. Hours cut from full-time to part-time. Told by my manager in person, no reason given. Note made.
> Early May. Asked human resources in writing about the hour change. No reply yet.

Notice what the summary does not do. It does not call anyone names, guess at motives, or say what the law is. It lists what happened and when, and each line points to a note or a copy.

## Preparing for a meeting with human resources

If you are invited to talk to human resources or an investigator, a little preparation helps you stay calm and clear.

- Bring your one-page summary and a copy of your written complaint.
- Decide the two or three most important points you want them to hear.
- Answer questions plainly. It is fine to say "I don't remember" or "I'd need to check my notes."
- Ask what happens next, who will contact you, and roughly when.
- Ask whether you can have a copy of any statement they write down for you to sign, and read it carefully before signing.
- Afterwards, send a short email summing up the meeting, and log it.

You can ask whether you may bring a support person. Some employers allow it and some do not. A union member can usually ask for a union representative.

## When you are ready to leave

Some people decide to stay and see whether things improve. Others decide to leave. Either choice can be the right one for you. If you are thinking of leaving, it is often wise to talk to an agency or a lawyer first, because the timing of a resignation, what you sign on the way out, and what you take with you can all matter. Do not sign a separation agreement or release without reading it carefully and, if you can, asking a lawyer about it.

# Looking after yourself while it is going on

Living through a hostile situation at work is draining. People describe trouble sleeping, dread on Sunday nights, headaches, losing interest in things they used to enjoy. You are not weak for feeling this.

Some things that help:

- **Tell someone you trust.** A friend, a family member, a counselor.
- **Use the employee assistance program.** Many employers offer free, confidential counseling through one. It is usually separate from your manager and human resources.
- **See a doctor or counselor if your health is affected.** Note visits and any time off in the health and time-off log.
- **Protect your time outside work.** Rest, movement, people who make you feel like yourself.
- **Plan for options.** Updating your résumé or quietly looking at other jobs is not giving up. It is giving yourself choices.

This book is not medical advice. If you ever feel unsafe or think about harming yourself, call or text the crisis line in your country, or call 911 in an emergency.

# Where to get help

You do not have to work this out alone. Help exists, much of it free.

- **Your company's human resources team or ethics line.** The first stop in most company policies.
- **A union representative**, if you have one.
- **The federal agency that enforces equal employment laws, and your state's fair employment agency.** These take complaints of discrimination and harassment. Many accept complaints online or by phone, and they can tell you the deadline that applies.
- **Your state labor department.** For questions about pay, hours, overtime and final paychecks.
- **Legal aid organizations.** Free or low-cost legal help for people with lower incomes. Some focus on workers.
- **The local bar association's lawyer referral service.** To find an employment lawyer. Many employment lawyers offer a free or low-cost first meeting.

The official sources at the back of this book list the agencies and deadlines for your state, each with the date it was checked.

## What to bring to a first meeting

Bring copies, never your only originals: your incident log, the checklist below with its boxes ticked, copies of what you were allowed to keep, your complaints and replies, and a one-page summary of the main events in date order.

# Checklist: papers to gather

Tick each group when you have a copy in your folder.

- [ ] The employee handbook and policies you were given
- [ ] Your offer letter, job description and performance reviews
- [ ] Pay stubs, schedules and your own record of hours
- [ ] Messages sent to you about the problem, kept as the rules allow
- [ ] Your dated incident notes
- [ ] Any complaint you made, and every reply
- [ ] Names of people who saw or heard what happened
- [ ] Notes of doctor or counselor visits and time off, if your health was affected

What matters differs by state and by situation. An agency or a lawyer can tell you what matters for yours.
