# Why you are not lazy

You know what you need to do. It is on a list somewhere, or in your head, or in the text you sent yourself last week. You have even worked out the first step. And still, for an hour or a day or a month, you cannot make yourself start.

Or you start, and halfway through you notice you are doing something else. Or you finish the thing and forget to send it. Or you promise a friend you will call on Thursday, and on Friday you remember.

People who live like this are often told they are lazy, disorganised or careless. Most of them are working harder than anyone around them, just to keep up.

## What "executive function" means

Executive function is the everyday name for a set of mental skills that let you manage yourself: planning, starting, switching between tasks, holding a plan in mind while you do it, keeping track of time, controlling impulses, and handling frustration well enough to keep going.

When these skills are under strain, people use the phrase executive dysfunction. It is not a diagnosis in itself. It shows up with ADHD, with depression and anxiety, after a brain injury, during grief, with burnout, with chronic illness and pain, with too little sleep, and simply in seasons of life when someone is carrying far too much at once. New parents, carers and people holding down two jobs know it well.

This book does not diagnose anything and is not a treatment. If you think a health condition is behind your struggles, talk to a doctor or a licensed clinician. Many people find that the right diagnosis and treatment help a great deal, and that tools like the ones in this book work better alongside them.

## Willpower is the wrong tool

Most advice for people who struggle to start and finish things is some version of "try harder". Make a stronger promise to yourself. Feel more guilty. Set a stricter alarm.

This rarely works for long, because the problem is not how much you want to do the thing. The problem is that one brain is being asked to do several jobs at once: remember everything, decide what matters, get started, stay on track, keep its spirits up, and check on itself afterwards. In a company, those would be several different people.

## The idea of this book

Imagine you had a small executive team, the kind a busy leader has. Each person on the team has one job:

- one keeps track of everything so nothing is forgotten;
- one decides each evening what tomorrow is for;
- one gets you moving when you are stuck;
- one checks in and holds you to what you said;
- one helps you handle the voice in your head that says you have already failed.

You cannot hire this team. But you can play each role on purpose, one at a time, with a page in this book for each. Instead of one exhausted brain trying to do everything at once, you switch hats. The hats do the remembering.

This is the idea behind A Player Mode: your own five-person executive team, running your day.

# Meet your five-person team

Here is your team. Each role gets its own chapter, with one tool and one page to fill in.

## The Executive Assistant

**Job:** get everything out of your head and into one trusted place, and keep the calendar honest.

Your brain is a poor filing cabinet. Every unfinished task you are trying to remember costs energy, even when you are not thinking about it. The Executive Assistant's job is to catch every task, appointment and idea in one list, and then to review it once a week so nothing slips.

## The Chief of Staff

**Job:** decide tomorrow, tonight.

Deciding what to do is one of the hardest parts of the day for a tired brain. The Chief of Staff does it the evening before, when the stakes feel lower: one thing that matters most, two smaller moves, and the very first step.

## The Executive Coach

**Job:** get you started when you cannot start.

Starting is where most people get stuck. The Coach does not lecture. It shrinks the first step until it is almost silly, changes the setting, sets a short timer, and gets you moving.

## The Accountability Partner

**Job:** turn a promise into something someone hears.

Promises made only to ourselves are easy to break quietly. The Accountability Partner checks in, kindly and briefly: what did you say you would do, what did you do, what got in the way, and what is the next promise, made a little smaller.

## The Mindset Coach

**Job:** answer the voice that says you have already failed.

After a lost morning, many people write off the whole day, or the whole week. The Mindset Coach catches the thought, checks it, and finds the next small action.

## How the team works together

You do not need all five on day one. Most people start with the Executive Assistant and the Chief of Staff, then add the others. By the end of this book you will have a simple daily rhythm:

- **Evening:** the Chief of Staff plans tomorrow in five minutes.
- **Morning:** you read the briefing and the Coach gets you started on the first step.
- **During the day:** the Assistant catches anything new; the Mindset Coach steps in if the day goes sideways.
- **Once a day or a few times a week:** the Accountability Partner checks in.
- **Once a week:** the Assistant runs the weekly review.

# The Executive Assistant: get it out of your head

## One capture list

Choose one place to catch everything: a small notebook you carry, a single note on your phone, or an app. One place. Not three.

Every time a task, idea, worry or appointment pops into your head, write it there straight away, in a few words. "Call dentist." "Birthday gift for Sam." "Ask boss about Friday." Do not organise it as you write. Just catch it.

At first the list will be long. That is fine. A long list on paper is far lighter than a long list in your head.

## The calendar is for appointments only

Put things on your calendar only if they happen at a set time: meetings, appointments, deadlines, pickups. Do not fill it with hopeful to-dos. A calendar full of tasks you never do teaches you to ignore it.

## Reminders that interrupt

If you forget things that happen at a set time, use reminders that interrupt you: an alarm, a phone notification with a sound, a note on the door. Set them for a few minutes before you need to leave, not at the time of the appointment.

## The two-minute rule

If something on your list takes about two minutes, such as answering a quick message or putting a form in an envelope, do it now instead of writing it down. It costs more to remember it than to do it.

## The weekly review

Once a week, at a set time, the Assistant sits down for fifteen to thirty minutes with the capture list and the calendar. Use the weekly review page at the back of this book:

- **Done.** Cross off what is finished. Notice it.
- **Moved.** What still matters but did not happen? Move it to next week, on purpose.
- **Dropped.** What no longer matters? Cross it out with relief. Dropping a task on purpose is a decision, not a failure.
- **Ahead.** Look at the next two weeks of the calendar. Any appointments that need preparation, travel or a reminder?

Many people find the weekly review is the single habit that helps most, because it stops the list from becoming a pile of guilt.

# The Chief of Staff: decide the day the night before

## Why the evening

In the morning, a tired brain faces a blank day and a long list, and spends its freshest energy deciding. Often it decides on whatever is loudest, or easiest, or nothing at all.

In the evening, the stakes feel lower. The Chief of Staff takes five minutes to decide what tomorrow is for, so that the morning you only has to follow the plan.

## The morning briefing

Use the morning briefing page at the back of this book. Fill it in the night before:

- **The one thing that matters most tomorrow.** If only one thing gets done, which one would make tomorrow a good day?
- **Two smaller moves.** Useful, but not the main event.
- **What could derail it.** A meeting that runs long, a child home sick, your phone. Naming it makes it easier to plan around.
- **The first step, under two minutes.** For the one thing, write the very first physical action. Not "write report" but "open the report file and type the title".

Then close the notebook. You are done for today.

## Protect the first hour

If you can, do the first step of the one thing early in the day, before email, messages and news. For many people the first hour is when they have the most control over their attention.

## Saying no

The Chief of Staff also guards your time. Before you agree to something new, ask: what would this replace? A short "Let me check and get back to you" gives you time to decide on purpose.

# The Executive Coach: starting when you can't start

Starting is the part people find hardest. You sit down, open the file, and your brain slides away. You tidy the desk. You check your phone. An hour passes.

The Coach has a small set of tools. Try one; if it does not work, try another.

## Make the first step tiny

The more vague or large a task, the harder it is to start. Make the first step so small it feels almost silly:

- not "clean the kitchen" but "put three things in the dishwasher";
- not "do the taxes" but "find last year's tax form and put it on the table";
- not "go for a run" but "put on running shoes".

Once you have started, carrying on is often easier.

## Use a short timer

Set a timer for ten minutes and tell yourself you can stop when it rings. Very often you will not want to. If you do stop, you still did ten minutes.

## Body doubling

Many people find it easier to work when someone else is nearby, even if that person is doing something completely different. This is sometimes called body doubling. Sit with a friend or family member while you both work. Go to a library or café. Join an online co-working session. The presence of another person seems to make starting easier.

## Change the place

If you cannot start at your desk, move. Kitchen table, a different room, outside. A new place can break the loop.

## Remove one obstacle

Ask: what is the one thing making this hard to start? Not knowing the first step? A missing password? A noisy room? A fear of doing it badly? Deal with that one thing.

## Start in the middle

If the beginning feels impossible, start somewhere else: the easiest section, the part you already know. There is no rule that says tasks must be done in order.

## The start log

Use the start log at the back of this book for a week. For each task you struggled to start, write what made it hard, the tiny step you took, and roughly how long it took to get moving. Patterns appear quickly: maybe you always stall on tasks that involve a phone call, or on anything first thing after lunch. Once you can see the pattern, you can plan for it.

# The Accountability Partner: a promise someone hears

## Why it helps

A promise you make only to yourself is easy to break without anyone noticing, including you. A promise someone else hears is different. You are more likely to keep it, and when you do not, you are more likely to notice and learn something.

## Choose a partner

An accountability partner can be a friend, a coworker, a sibling, a coach, or an app that checks in. The role is not to nag or judge. It is to ask three simple questions, kindly and briefly.

## The check-in

Use the check-in page at the back of this book. Once a day or a few times a week:

- **What did I say I would do?**
- **What did I do?**
- **What got in the way?**
- **What is my next promise, made smaller?**

The last question matters most. If you said you would write for an hour and wrote for ten minutes, the next promise is not "two hours to catch up". It is "twenty minutes tomorrow at nine". Smaller promises, kept, rebuild trust in yourself.

## Track without shame

Some people like a simple tracker: a grid of days with a mark for each day the promise was kept. If you use one, never use it to punish yourself. A missed day is information, not a verdict.

## If you do not have a partner

Write your check-in anyway, as if you were telling someone. Many people find that writing it down does much of the work.

# The Mindset Coach: when your head says you've already failed

Everyone has a voice in their head. For people who have struggled for years with getting things done, that voice can be harsh. "You always do this." "You'll never change." "The day's ruined, so why bother."

The Mindset Coach does not try to silence the voice or replace it with forced positivity. It checks what the voice says, the way a good coach checks the facts.

## The thought check

This tool is in the style of cognitive behavioral approaches used by many therapists. It is not therapy, but many people find it a useful habit. Use the thought check page at the back of this book:

- **Situation.** What happened? Just the facts. "I spent the morning on my phone and didn't start the report."
- **The thought.** What did the voice say? "I'm hopeless. I'll never get this done. I might as well give up."
- **What it made me do.** "I felt awful and avoided the report all afternoon too."
- **A truer thought.** Not a cheerful lie, but a more accurate one. "I lost a morning. I've lost mornings before and still finished things. I have the afternoon."
- **One next action.** "Open the report and write one paragraph."

## Common thinking traps

Some patterns show up again and again:

- **All or nothing.** "If I can't do it properly, there's no point."
- **Fortune telling.** "This will go badly."
- **Labelling.** "I'm lazy" instead of "I didn't start that task today".
- **Writing off the day.** "The morning's gone, so the day's gone."

Naming the trap is often enough to loosen its grip.

## The lost-day reset

When a day has gone sideways, try this short reset instead of writing it off:

- Stand up, drink a glass of water, step outside or open a window.
- Look at the morning briefing. Is the one thing still possible in a smaller form?
- Pick the first step of that smaller version and do it.
- If the day truly cannot be saved, plan tomorrow with the Chief of Staff, and rest.

A day with one small thing done is not a lost day.

## Be your own good boss

Imagine the kindest, fairest boss you ever had, or would like to have. How would they talk to a hard-working employee who had a rough morning? Talk to yourself like that.

# Putting the team to work for one week

Here is a simple plan to try the whole team for seven days. Use the pages at the back of this book.

## Day 1: the Assistant

Start your capture list. Spend fifteen minutes emptying your head into it: every task, worry and errand you can think of. Put real appointments on the calendar. In the evening, choose a time for your weekly review and put it on the calendar too.

## Day 2: the Chief of Staff

In the evening, fill in your first morning briefing: the one thing, two smaller moves, what could derail it, and the first step.

## Day 3: the Coach

In the morning, do the first step of the one thing before you open messages. Use a ten-minute timer. Write one line in the start log.

## Day 4: the Accountability Partner

Tell someone what your one thing is today. In the evening, fill in a check-in, out loud or on paper.

## Day 5: the Mindset Coach

At some point today, notice the harsh voice. Fill in one thought check.

## Day 6: rest and notice

Do the morning briefing and the start log as usual. Notice which roles felt natural and which felt hard.

## Day 7: the weekly review

Do your first weekly review. Done, moved, dropped, ahead. Then decide which roles you want to keep for next week.

## After the first week

Keep what helped. Drop what did not. Most people end up with a small daily rhythm: an evening briefing, a tiny first step in the morning, a weekly review. That rhythm, kept up imperfectly, does more than any burst of motivation.

## When to ask for more help

If you have tried these tools and still struggle, or if the struggle is affecting your work, relationships or health, talk to a doctor or licensed clinician. There is no prize for doing it alone.
