# Start here: papers and dates, not predictions

A VA disability rating is decided from paper. The people deciding your claim never meet you in the way a doctor does. They read your service records, your medical records, the report from your exam, statements from people who know you, and what you send them. If a paper is missing, it cannot count.

That is why so much of a claim comes down to gathering, copying and keeping papers in order. It is slow, dull work, often done while you are dealing with pain, sleep problems or a full-time job. This binder breaks it into steps.

## What this book does

- Explains in plain words how a rating is decided.
- Lists the papers that usually matter, and how to get copies.
- Helps you prepare for the exam, and describe your bad days honestly.
- Explains statements from people who know you.
- Walks through the choices after a decision.
- Gives you logs to track appointments, everything you send, statements and symptoms.
- Lists free, accredited help, and how to avoid people who charge for it.

## What this book does not do

It does not predict your rating. Nobody honest can do that from a book.

It is not affiliated with the Department of Veterans Affairs, and it does not prepare, file or review claims.

It is not legal or medical advice. Rules and forms change; the official VA website and an accredited representative have the final word.

## A word on free help

You never have to pay anyone to help you file a VA disability claim. Accredited veterans service officers, who work for veterans service organizations, states and counties, help for free. The official sources at the back of this book list where to find them in your state. Chapter seven explains how to tell accredited helpers from people who charge.

## The family's role

Many veterans are helped by a spouse, a parent or an adult child. If that is you, this binder is for you too. You can keep the logs, make the copies and sit in on appointments where the veteran wants you there. A family member can also write a statement about what they have seen.

# How a rating is decided, in plain words

You do not need to understand every rule to file a good claim. But knowing the basic pieces helps you see why each paper matters.

## Three questions the VA asks

For each condition you claim, the VA is broadly asking three things:

- **Do you have the condition now?** Usually shown by a current diagnosis in medical records or an exam.
- **Did something happen in service?** An injury, an illness, an exposure, an event, shown in service records or other papers.
- **Is there a link between the two?** Usually a medical opinion that the current condition is at least as likely as not connected to service.

When all three are present, the condition is called service-connected.

Some conditions are presumed to be connected for certain veterans, for example some illnesses linked to particular exposures or places and times of service. If one applies to you, the link may not need to be shown in the same way. An accredited representative can tell you whether any presumption fits your service.

## The rating schedule

Once a condition is service-connected, the VA rates how much it affects you using a published rating schedule. The schedule lists conditions and describes symptoms and limits at each level, from zero up to one hundred percent, in steps of ten. A zero rating means the condition is service-connected but not rated as disabling right now; it still matters, because it can be increased later if it gets worse.

The descriptions in the schedule are why your medical records and your exam matter so much. The VA compares what the records say about your symptoms with the words in the schedule.

## Combined ratings

If you have more than one rated condition, the VA does not simply add the percentages together. It combines them in a way that accounts for the part of you each condition affects, so the combined rating is usually lower than the sum. The VA publishes how this works on its website. You do not need to calculate it yourself, but it helps to know so the final number does not come as a shock.

## Effective dates

The date your benefits start is called the effective date. It is often tied to the date the VA received your claim, or the date you told the VA you intended to file. Telling the VA early that you intend to file can protect an earlier date while you gather papers. The VA's own pages explain how long that protection lasts; check them, or ask an accredited representative, early in the process.

# Service papers and records

Your service papers are the foundation of your claim. They show who you are, when and where you served, and often what happened to you.

## The core papers

- **Your discharge papers.** The DD two-fourteen (your discharge form), or the equivalent for your branch and era. Keep several copies. The original goes somewhere safe, such as a fireproof box.
- **Service treatment records.** Your medical records from your time in service: sick-call visits, injuries, profiles, hospital stays.
- **Personnel records.** Assignments, deployments, awards, evaluations. These can help show where you were and what you did.
- **Unit records or reports**, for events such as an accident or an incident, when they mention you.

## How to get copies

Your service records are kept by the military and the national archives. Veterans and their next of kin can request copies through the official request process listed in the official sources at the back of this book. Requests can take time, so ask early.

If your service treatment records are incomplete, do not give up. Other papers can fill gaps: letters home, photographs from the time, statements from people who served with you, records from a civilian doctor you saw soon after leaving service.

## Keeping them safe

- Make copies before you send anything to anyone. Never send your only original.
- Keep a list of what you have, using the checklist at the back.
- Scan or photograph each paper and keep the files in a second place you control.
- When you send something, log it in the "What I sent the VA" log the same day.

# Medical records and the exam

Medical records describe your condition today. The exam, which the VA often orders for a claim, is where a clinician looks at you and writes a report the rater relies on.

## Gathering your medical records

- **VA medical records**, if you get care at the VA. You can see many of them online through your VA health account, and request a full copy.
- **Private records** from your own doctors, hospitals, therapists and specialists. Ask each office for a copy of your records. Most can send them electronically. You can also authorize the VA to request them, but having your own copy means you know what is in it.
- **Pharmacy records**, which show what you have been prescribed over time.

Read your records. If something important is missing, such as a visit about the condition you are claiming, ask the office about it.

## Before the exam

The exam is sometimes called the C&P exam. It may be done by a VA clinician or by a contractor the VA uses. Go to it. Missing it without a good reason can harm your claim; if you cannot attend, contact the office in advance and ask to reschedule.

Before you go:

- Read your own records for each condition you claimed, so you can describe your history.
- Use the symptoms log at the back of this book for a few weeks before the exam. Note your bad days, not only your good ones.
- Write a short list for each condition: when it started, what it stops you doing, how often it flares, what treatment you have had, how it affects work and home life.
- Bring a list of your medicines.

## At the exam

- Be honest and specific. Do not exaggerate, and do not play things down.
- Describe your worst days as well as an average day. Many veterans say "I'm fine" out of habit. If you are not fine on bad days, say so.
- If a movement hurts, say so and stop. Do not push through pain to look tough.
- Give examples: "On a bad day I can't stand long enough to cook a meal." "I miss about two days of work a month" is the kind of detail that helps, written in words in your notes.
- If you bring someone with you, ask whether they may come into the room.

## After the exam

As soon as you can, write down in the appointments log: the date, the place, the examiner's name, what you told them, and how long the exam lasted. You can later ask for a copy of the exam report. If it contains clear mistakes, an accredited representative can help you respond.

# Building your file, one condition at a time

A claim often covers several conditions: a knee, hearing, sleep, back pain, mental health. Each one is decided on its own papers. The simplest way to stay organised is to give each condition its own section in your binder, with the same five parts.

## The five parts for each condition

- **Today.** The papers that show you have the condition now: a diagnosis, recent treatment notes, test results.
- **In service.** The papers that show what happened in service: a sick-call note, an injury report, a deployment record, a profile, a statement from someone who was there.
- **The link.** Any medical opinion connecting the two. If you do not have one, write "none yet" so you can see the gap.
- **How it affects you.** Your symptoms log, statements from family or coworkers, notes about missed work.
- **What I sent and what came back.** Copies of anything about this condition that went to the VA, and every letter that came back.

When a section is thin, you know where to look next. When all five parts have something in them, the condition is ready to be described clearly to your service officer.

## Common reasons claims stall

Service officers see the same problems again and again. Most are about paper.

- **No current diagnosis.** A condition you have never been treated for is hard to rate. If you have symptoms, see a doctor and get it in your records.
- **Nothing from service.** If your service records do not mention the event, look for other papers: letters, photos, statements from people who served with you, records from a doctor you saw soon after you got out.
- **No link.** A diagnosis and an in-service event are not enough on their own; the claim usually needs a medical opinion connecting them. Ask your service officer how these are usually obtained.
- **A missed exam.** If you cannot attend, ask to reschedule before the date.
- **Mail that went to an old address.** Keep your address up to date with the VA, and check your online VA account for letters.
- **A deadline that passed.** Write every letter's date in your log the day it arrives.
- **An exam that understated your worst days.** Use the symptoms log so you can describe your bad days with real examples.

None of these is a reason to give up. Each one has a next step, and an accredited representative can help you take it.

## Keeping one master list

On the first page of your binder, keep one short list: each condition, the date you claimed it, its status, and the date of the last letter about it. Update it each time something changes. When someone asks "where is your claim?", you can answer in a minute.

# Statements from people who know

People who see your condition affect your life can describe it in a written statement. These are often called buddy statements, though anyone can write one: a spouse, a parent, a friend, a coworker, someone you served with.

## Who can write one

- **Someone who served with you**, about an event in service, an injury, or how you changed.
- **A family member or partner**, about how the condition affects you at home: sleep, mood, pain, what you can no longer do.
- **A coworker or boss**, about how it affects your work: missed days, changes in duties.
- **A friend**, about changes they have seen over time.

## What makes a statement useful

- The writer's full name and how they know you.
- What they saw or heard themselves, with rough dates.
- Specific examples, not general praise.
- How often things happen.
- A signature and date, and the statement on the VA's own lay or witness statement form if possible, or with the certification the VA asks for.

An invented example of a useful paragraph:

> I have been married to him for many years. Since he came home, he wakes up most nights shouting, and some nights he sleeps on the couch so he does not wake me. He no longer goes to our children's games because crowds make him leave within a few minutes. Last month he left our daughter's school concert after the first song.

## Keep copies and a list

Log each statement in the statements log: who wrote it, how they know you, what they saw, the date and where the copy is kept.

Never write a statement for someone else to sign, and never ask anyone to say something they did not see. Statements must be true and in the writer's own words.

# After the decision: increase, supplemental claim or review

When the VA decides your claim, it sends a decision letter. Read the whole letter. It explains which conditions were granted or denied, at what rating, and the reasons. The reasons tell you what the rater saw and what was missing.

## If you agree

Keep the letter with your papers. If a condition gets worse later, you can ask for an increase.

## If a condition gets worse: asking for an increase

If a rated condition worsens, you can file a new claim for an increased rating. You will usually need recent medical records showing the change, and you may be sent for a new exam. Your symptoms log is especially useful here.

## If you disagree: the review lanes

The VA offers several ways to have a decision looked at again. Each has its own form and deadline, and the official VA pages explain them in full. In plain words:

- **Supplemental claim.** You add new and relevant information that was not considered before, such as a new medical opinion or a new statement, and ask the VA to decide again.
- **Higher-level review.** A more senior reviewer looks at the same file again, without new papers, to see whether there was an error.
- **Board appeal.** You ask the Board of Veterans' Appeals to review the decision, with different options for whether you add new papers or ask for a hearing.

Which lane fits depends on why the claim was denied or rated as it was. This is exactly the kind of choice an accredited representative can help with, for free.

## Keep the dates

Every review lane has a time limit, counted from the date of the decision letter. Write the date of each letter in your log the day it arrives, and find out the deadline early.

# Free help and the people to avoid

## Accredited help is free

The VA accredits three kinds of people to help with claims:

- **Veterans service officers**, who work for veterans service organizations, state veterans offices and many county offices. Their help is free.
- **Attorneys** and **claims agents**, who may charge fees only in certain situations and within limits the VA sets, generally not for an initial claim.

The VA's website has a search tool to check whether someone is accredited. The official sources at the back of this book list free help in your state.

## Warning signs

Some companies charge veterans large fees, or a share of future benefits, for help with initial claims, sometimes calling themselves coaches or consultants. Be careful if anyone:

- is not accredited by the VA;
- asks you to sign a contract to pay them a percentage of your back pay or future benefits for an initial claim;
- promises a specific rating or a fast result;
- asks for your VA account login;
- tells you to send papers to them instead of to the VA.

If you are unsure, ask your state veterans office or a veterans service organization before you sign anything.

## Looking after yourself

Claims can stir up memories and stress. If you are struggling, the Veterans Crisis Line is available any time by phone, text or chat; dial the national crisis line number and press one. In an emergency, call 911.
