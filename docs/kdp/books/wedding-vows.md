# Before you start: what vows are for

Wedding vows are promises, said out loud, in front of the people who matter to you. That is all. They do not need to be poetry. They do not need to be long. They do not need to make anyone cry, though they often do.

Most people find vows hard to write for three reasons:

- **The blank page.** It is hard to know where to start.
- **The pressure.** It feels as if these words have to sum up a whole relationship.
- **The audience.** Saying something private in front of a room is frightening.

This book deals with all three. It gives you a simple shape, a set of prompts to find your own material, examples that show what the shape sounds like, a way to edit, and help with saying them out loud. Set aside one evening. By the end, you will have vows.

## Agree on the basics first

Before either of you writes a word, agree on four things with your partner:

- **Length.** A rough length for both of you, so one person does not read three sentences while the other reads three pages. Somewhere between one and two minutes each, spoken, is common.
- **Tone.** Serious, funny, or both? It is fine for your styles to differ, but avoid one person doing a comedy set while the other pours their heart out.
- **Secret or shared.** Will you hear each other's vows for the first time at the ceremony, or read them to each other first? Either is fine.
- **Approval.** Check with your officiant that personal vows are welcome, and how they fit into the ceremony.

# The simple shape

Most vows that work follow a shape with four parts. You do not need to use all four, and they can come in any order, but this order is easy to follow.

## The four parts

- **Who you are to me.** Something true and specific about your partner. What you love, admire or noticed first.
- **A small story.** A moment that shows it. One or two sentences, not a whole tale.
- **What I promise.** Your actual promises. Some big, some small.
- **Our life together.** A closing line that looks forward.

## The shape in action

An invented example, with each part marked in your head as you read:

> When I first met you, you were arguing with a vending machine, and losing. I remember thinking that anyone who could laugh that hard at themselves was someone I wanted to know.
>
> Since then I have watched you stay up all night with a sick friend, learn to cook my grandmother's soup, and carry our dog up three flights of stairs.
>
> I promise to keep laughing with you, especially at myself. I promise to tell you the truth, even when it is hard. I promise to make the coffee on weekdays, and to let you win at cards at least some of the time.
>
> I can't wait to see who we become together. I choose you, today and every day.

That is about one minute, spoken slowly.

## Why the shape works

Specific details make vows feel personal, because they could only be about the two of you. Promises give the vows their weight. A short story makes the room smile or tear up. A forward-looking ending gives everyone a moment to breathe before the next part of the ceremony.

# Forty prompts to find your words

Do not write vows yet. First, gather material. Take a notebook, set a timer for twenty minutes, and answer as many of these as you can in a few words each. Do not edit. Go for quantity.

## About your partner

- What did you notice about them the first time you met?
- When did you know this was serious?
- What do they do that no one else does?
- What do they do when they think no one is watching?
- What do your friends say about them?
- What are they surprisingly good at?
- What makes them laugh hardest?
- How do they show love?
- What have they taught you?
- When are they most themselves?

## About your story

- Where did you meet, and what happened?
- What was your first date like? What went wrong?
- What was the first trip you took together?
- What is a small, ordinary moment you love?
- What is a hard time you got through together?
- What is an inside joke you can explain in one sentence?
- What is a habit of theirs you first found strange and now love?
- When did they surprise you?
- When did you feel most looked after by them?
- What song, food or place belongs to the two of you?

## About your promises

- What do you want to keep doing for them, forever?
- What do you want to get better at?
- What will you do when you disagree?
- What will you do when they are sick, sad or scared?
- What small daily things will you promise?
- What big things will you promise?
- What will you protect, about them or about the two of you?
- What will you never stop saying?
- What do you hope they can always count on?
- What promise would make them laugh?

## About your future

- Where do you picture yourselves in many years?
- What adventure do you want to have together?
- What do you want your home to feel like?
- What do you want to build together?
- What tradition do you want to start?
- If you have or want children, what do you hope they see in the two of you?
- What are you most looking forward to?
- What do you want to remember about today?
- What does "forever" mean to you?
- If you could say only one sentence, what would it be?

Look back over your answers. Circle the ones that made you smile, or made your throat tighten. Those are your material.

# Examples in different voices

These examples are invented. They are here to show how the shape sounds in different styles. Do not copy them. Read them, notice what you like, and then write your own.

## Classic and heartfelt

> I stand here today because you made me believe in a love that is patient and kind and real. You have been my calm in every storm and my reason to laugh on ordinary days. I promise to love you when it is easy and when it is hard, to listen with an open heart, and to build a home where we are both free to grow. I give you my hand, my heart and my whole life. Today and always.

## Funny, with heart

> I would like to begin by apologizing to everyone here for how many times I have described this person as "perfect" in the last two years. I stand by it. You laugh at my terrible jokes, you remember my mother's birthday when I forget, and you have never once complained about my snoring, at least not to my face. I promise to keep telling the jokes, to set a reminder for my mother's birthday, and to consider seeing someone about the snoring. Most of all, I promise to love you loudly and without reservation for the rest of our lives.

## Short and simple

> You are my favorite person. I promise to be kind to you, honest with you and on your side. Let's grow old together.

## Faith-friendly

> I thank God for bringing you into my life. I promise to love you as I have been loved, with patience, forgiveness and faithfulness. I promise to pray with you and for you, to serve you, and to build a home where our faith can grow. With God's help, I will be yours for as long as we both shall live.

## Second marriage, or blending a family

> We both came here with histories, and I would not change either of ours, because they led us to this room. I promise to honor what came before and to build something new with you. To your children, who are standing here with us: I promise to be someone you can count on, to respect your place in your parent's heart, and to make room at our table for whoever you become.

## Renewing your vows

> When we first said these words, we had no idea what was coming: the moves, the babies, the long nights, the laughter. You have kept every promise you made me, and some you never said out loud. I promise to keep choosing you, with even more gratitude and a lot more gray hair. I would marry you again tomorrow.

# More examples, and the words around the vows

## For a nervous speaker

If speaking in front of people terrifies you, keep your vows very short and very true. Three sentences, said slowly while looking at your partner, can be the most moving moment of the day.

> I'm not good at speeches, so I'll keep this simple. You make me braver than I am. I promise to love you, to look after you, and to be on your team for the rest of our lives.

## When you both write together

Some couples write one set of vows together and say them in turns, or say the same promises to each other.

> We promise to tell each other the truth. We promise to say sorry and mean it. We promise to keep making each other laugh. We promise to build a home where friends are welcome and the door is always open. We promise to choose each other, every day.

## Mixing in a second language

If you or your partner grew up with another language, you might say a line or two in it, then repeat it in English so everyone understands. A short phrase from a parent's or grandparent's language can mean a great deal to the family in the room.

## Ring exchange words

Many ceremonies include a short line as you place the ring. Your officiant may have a traditional version. Simple examples:

> With this ring, I marry you, and I give you my heart.

> I give you this ring as a sign of my love and my promise. Wear it and remember that you are loved.

> This ring is round, with no end. So is my love for you.

## Answering the officiant

In most ceremonies, the officiant also asks each of you a question, such as whether you take your partner as your spouse. You answer with "I do" or "I will". Check with your officiant which words they use, so you know when it is your turn.

## Promises people often include

If you are stuck for promises, here are some that couples often use. Pick the ones that are true for you and put them in your own words.

- To listen before I answer.
- To keep dating you.
- To support your dreams, even the ones I don't understand yet.
- To be patient when you're tired, and to ask for patience when I am.
- To never go to sleep angry, or at least to try.
- To make you laugh on bad days.
- To take care of you when you are sick.
- To celebrate your wins as if they were mine.
- To grow with you, and to let you grow.
- To be honest, even when it is hard.
- To share the remote, the blanket and the last piece of cake.
- To keep choosing us.

## Words to avoid on the day

- Long lists of your partner's past relationships, or your own.
- Promises you cannot keep, said to get a laugh.
- Anything about other guests that might embarrass them.
- Private details your partner has not agreed to share.

# Writing your draft

Now you write. Give yourself thirty minutes.

## Step by step

- Pick one detail about your partner from your prompt answers. Write two sentences about it. That is your opening.
- Pick one small story. Tell it in no more than three sentences.
- Pick three to five promises. Mix big and small. Write each as "I promise to…".
- Write one closing line that looks forward.

Read it out loud once, slowly, with a timer. Most people are surprised how long or short it is.

## If you are stuck

- Write the vows as a letter first: "Dear…". Then cut the letter down.
- Talk your vows into your phone's voice recorder, as if speaking to a friend about your partner. Then type up the strongest lines.
- Write the worst possible vows, full of clichés, as a joke. Then write the opposite.

# Editing: cutting it down and making it yours

Your first draft is probably too long, too general, or both. That is normal.

## Cut

- Cut any sentence that could be said about anyone. "You are my everything" could be. "You learned to cook my grandmother's soup" could not.
- Cut the second example when one will do.
- Cut long stories down to their key moment.
- Cut apologies about your writing.

## Replace clichés

Common phrases like "my rock", "my other half", "soulmate" and "partner in crime" are not wrong, but they are not yours. Try replacing each one with a specific detail that shows the same thing.

## Check the inside jokes

An inside joke can be wonderful, but only if the room can understand it, or if it takes one sentence to explain. If half the guests will be confused, cut it or explain it briefly.

## Check for anything private

Ask yourself: would my partner be comfortable with every word of this said in front of their grandmother and their boss? If not, save that line for a private letter.

## Read it aloud again

Read it aloud. Listen for sentences that are hard to say, words you trip over, and places where you run out of breath. Shorten them.

# Saying them out loud

Many people worry more about saying their vows than writing them.

## Practise

- Read your vows out loud at least five times in the days before the wedding.
- Practise standing up, holding the card you will use.
- Record yourself once on your phone and listen back.

## Print them well

- Print or neatly write your vows on a card or in a small booklet, not your phone. A phone can light up with messages at the wrong moment, and it does not look lovely in photos.
- Use large type and short lines so you can glance down easily.
- Number the cards if there is more than one.
- Give a copy to your officiant or a trusted guest in case you forget yours.

## On the day

- Breathe before you start.
- Look up at your partner between sentences.
- Speak slower than feels natural.
- If you cry, stop, breathe, smile and carry on. Everyone understands.
- If you lose your place, look down, find it, and keep going. No one minds.

## Keep them

After the wedding, put both sets of vows somewhere safe: a frame, a box, the back of your wedding album. Many couples read them again on anniversaries.
