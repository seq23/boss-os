# You said yes. Now what?

Being asked to be maid of honor is one of the warmest compliments a friend or sister can give you. It is also a job, and nobody hands you the job description.

This handbook is that description. It covers the whole role, from the day you say yes to the last song of the night, with checklists and fill-in pages so you can keep track without losing your mind, your budget or your friendship.

It works the same way whether you are the maid of honor, the matron of honor (the traditional name when the honor attendant is married), or anyone standing beside the couple, whatever they call you.

## What the role usually involves

Every wedding is different, and the couple decides what they want. But the role usually includes some of these:

- being a steady, trusted friend to the person getting married;
- helping with planning tasks they ask for;
- leading the other bridesmaids or attendants and keeping them informed;
- planning or co-planning the bridal shower and the bachelorette;
- helping with the dress, and on the day, with the dress again;
- holding the bouquet during the vows, and sometimes the rings;
- signing the marriage license as a witness, in some places;
- giving a toast at the reception, at many weddings;
- keeping calm and solving small problems on the day.

## What it does not involve

- Paying for the wedding.
- Agreeing to every idea without question.
- Planning things the couple did not ask for.
- Being available every hour of every day.
- Giving up your own life, health or budget.

## Talk early

Within a week or two of saying yes, have a relaxed conversation with the person getting married. Ask:

- What do you most want help with?
- What do you want to do yourself?
- Are you hoping for a shower, a bachelorette, both or neither?
- What's your vision for the day?
- How do you want me to work with the other attendants?
- Is there anything you're worried about?

Write the answers on the planning page at the back of this book. They will save you guessing for the next year.

# Talking money early

Money is the part of the role nobody wants to talk about and everyone needs to.

## What attendants often pay for

Customs vary by family, culture and region, but attendants often pay for some of these:

- their own outfit, shoes and alterations;
- hair and makeup, unless the couple covers it;
- travel and accommodation for the wedding;
- a share of the shower and bachelorette;
- a gift for the couple.

For a destination wedding or a long bachelorette trip, these add up quickly.

## Have the conversation

Before plans are made, talk openly with the couple and the other attendants about what things are likely to cost. It is far easier to set a budget at the start than to say no to a trip that is already booked.

Some ways to say it:

> I want to make this amazing for you. To plan well, it would help to know what everyone can spend, so nobody is stretched.

> I'd love to come on the trip, but it's more than I can do this year. Could we plan a local night too?

## Splitting shared costs

If attendants share costs for the shower or bachelorette, decide at the start how costs will be split, who collects money, and when. Keep a simple record of who paid what using the budget page at the back of this book. Send a short summary to everyone so there are no surprises.

## Your limits are allowed

If you cannot afford something, say so kindly and early. A good friend would rather know than have you go into debt.

# The months before: dress, details and the group chat

## Dress shopping

Many couples want their maid of honor at dress appointments.

- Ask how many people they want there. Fewer is usually calmer.
- Be honest but kind. Ask what they love about a dress before saying what you think.
- Watch their face. They often know before anyone says a word.
- Help with the practical questions: delivery dates, alteration costs, and what fits the venue and season.

## Your own outfit

If the couple chooses your dress, order early, and leave time for alterations. If they let you choose within a color or style, check with them before buying.

## The group chat

A group chat for the attendants is useful and can easily become overwhelming.

- Keep it for wedding logistics.
- Post decisions clearly: dates, costs, deadlines, what to bring.
- Pin the important messages.
- Keep negative comments about the couple, their choices or anyone else out of the chat. Screenshots travel.
- If someone is upset, talk to them privately.

## Being a steady friend

Planning a wedding is stressful. Sometimes the most useful thing you can do has nothing to do with the wedding: a walk, a film, a night where nobody mentions the seating chart.

# Planning the bridal shower

The shower is often the biggest planning job a maid of honor takes on. Sometimes a parent, relative or friend hosts instead; agree who is in charge at the start.

## The basics to decide

- **Who hosts and who pays.** The host or hosts usually cover the cost.
- **Date.** Often a few weeks to a few months before the wedding.
- **Guest list.** Ask the couple for it. Guests at the shower are usually also invited to the wedding.
- **Place.** A home, a garden, a restaurant's private room, a tea room.
- **Style.** Brunch, afternoon tea, a backyard party, a mixed-gender celebration.
- **Budget.** Set it before you book anything.

## A simple shower plan

- Send invitations a few weeks ahead. Include the registry if the couple has one.
- Plan simple food: a brunch spread, finger sandwiches, a cake.
- Decorate lightly: flowers, a sign, a photo table.
- Plan one or two short activities, then leave time for people to talk.
- Have someone write down each gift and who gave it, so the couple can send thank-you notes.
- Plan the order: arrival and food, a game or two, gifts, cake, goodbyes.

## Games that do not embarrass anyone

Choose activities that the guest of honor will enjoy and that work for grandmothers and coworkers alike.

- **How well do you know the couple?** A short quiz about how they met and their favorite things.
- **Advice cards.** Guests write a piece of advice or a wish for the couple.
- **Recipe cards.** Guests bring or write a favorite recipe for the couple's kitchen.
- **Date night jar.** Guests write date ideas on slips of paper for the couple's first year.
- **Wedding bingo during gifts.** Guests mark off squares as gifts are opened.

Avoid anything that puts the guest of honor or guests on the spot in a way they may not like. When in doubt, ask.

# Planning the bachelorette

## Ask what they want

Some people want a weekend away. Some want a quiet dinner. Some want a dance party, a spa day, a hike, a cooking class or no bachelorette at all. Ask before you plan.

## Plan around the group

- Ask for dates and budgets early.
- Choose something most of the group can afford, and make it fine for someone to join only part of it.
- If there is drinking, make sure everyone has a safe way home.
- Keep the guest of honor's comfort first. If they would hate a surprise, there should be no surprise.

## A simple one-night plan

- Dinner at a restaurant with a private table.
- A short activity: a class, karaoke, a show.
- Drinks or dessert somewhere with music.
- A plan to get everyone home safely.

## A weekend plan

- Book accommodation early and collect deposits.
- Plan one main activity each day and leave time to rest.
- Share a short itinerary in the group chat.
- Bring snacks, water and a small first-aid kit.

# Writing and giving the toast

Many maids of honor give a toast at the reception. It is often the part people dread most. A good toast is short, warm, specific, and about the couple, not about you.

## How long

About two to three minutes, spoken. That is usually three to five short paragraphs. Shorter is almost always better.

## The shape

- **Hello and who you are.** "Hi everyone, I'm Jess, and I've been Maya's friend since we were eleven."
- **A short story about the person you are there for.** One story that shows who they are.
- **How their partner fits.** When you met their partner, or when you knew they were right.
- **A wish for the couple.** What you hope for them.
- **The toast.** Ask everyone to raise a glass and say the couple's names.

## The toast builder

Fill in the toast builder page at the back of this book, one line at a time. Then read it aloud and time it.

## What to leave out

- Stories about past partners.
- Embarrassing stories the couple would not want their grandparents to hear.
- Inside jokes that need a long explanation.
- Long lists of thanks; the couple or hosts usually do that.
- Too many drinks before you speak.

## An invented example

> Hi everyone. I'm Jess, Maya's friend since sixth grade, when she sat next to me on the bus and offered me half her sandwich. She has been offering people half of everything ever since: her time, her clothes, her couch, and a lot of unasked-for advice.
>
> When Maya met Sam, she called me and said, "He's very calm. I don't know what to do with that." Over the next few years I watched Sam be calm when her car broke down, when her flight was cancelled, and when she decided to repaint the whole apartment on a Sunday night. And I watched Maya become even more herself.
>
> Sam, thank you for loving my friend the way she deserves. Maya, thank you for letting me stand next to you today.
>
> Please raise your glasses. To Maya and Sam.

## Delivering it

- Print it on a card in large type. Not your phone.
- Practise out loud several times.
- Hold the microphone close to your mouth.
- Speak slower than you think you need to.
- Look at the couple during the last part.

# The wedding day: your hour-by-hour checklist

## The day before

- [ ] Confirm your arrival time and where to go
- [ ] Charge your phone and bring a charger
- [ ] Pack your outfit, shoes and accessories
- [ ] Pack the emergency kit
- [ ] Print your toast
- [ ] Get some sleep

## Getting ready

- [ ] Arrive on time, or early
- [ ] Bring snacks and water for the getting-ready room
- [ ] Keep the timeline in your pocket
- [ ] Keep the room calm: music, kind words, no drama
- [ ] Help with the dress, buttons and veil
- [ ] Make sure the person getting married eats something

## The ceremony

- [ ] Make sure rings, the license and vows cards are with the right people
- [ ] Walk in when cued
- [ ] Hold the bouquet during the vows
- [ ] Fix the dress or train before the walk back up the aisle
- [ ] Sign the license as a witness, if asked

## The reception

- [ ] Help gather people for photos
- [ ] Give your toast
- [ ] Keep an eye on the couple: water, food, a quiet moment
- [ ] Dance
- [ ] Help collect gifts, cards and personal items at the end

## Emergency kit

- [ ] Safety pins and a small sewing kit
- [ ] Stain remover pen
- [ ] Tissues
- [ ] Pain relievers and bandages
- [ ] Mints
- [ ] Hair ties, pins and a small brush
- [ ] Lip balm and powder
- [ ] Phone charger
- [ ] Snacks and a water bottle
- [ ] Clear nail polish for tights
- [ ] Fashion tape
- [ ] Flat shoes for later

# When things go wrong

Something always goes a little wrong at a wedding. That is normal.

## When attendants clash

Talk to people privately, not in the group chat. Listen first. Remind everyone what the day is about. If you need to, ask the couple how they would like it handled, but try not to add to their stress.

## When the couple is stressed

Listen without fixing. Take one task off their plate. Remind them to eat, sleep and spend time together that is not about the wedding.

## When something goes wrong on the day

A late vendor, a torn hem, rain. Solve what you can quietly, tell the couple only what they need to know, and keep smiling. Very few guests notice what goes wrong; they notice how people react.

## When you have reached your limit

You are allowed to have a life outside the wedding. If the role is taking more time, money or energy than you can give, say so honestly and kindly. Offer what you can do instead.
