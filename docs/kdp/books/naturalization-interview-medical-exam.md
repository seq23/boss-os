# About this guide

On the road to a green card and, later, to citizenship, two appointments make many people nervous: the immigration medical exam with a civil surgeon, and the naturalization interview. Both are important. Both are also very predictable once you know what happens.

This guide walks you through each one in plain words: what to gather, what to bring, what happens in the room, and what comes after. It ends with checklists and a log for your appointments and mail.

## What this guide is, and is not

This is an independent guide. It is not affiliated with U.S. Citizenship and Immigration Services, the State Department or any government agency, and it is not an official publication.

It is educational only. It is not legal advice and not medical advice. Immigration rules, forms, fees and procedures change, sometimes with little notice. The official immigration website and the instructions that come with each form have the final word. For advice about your own case, speak to an immigration attorney or an accredited representative from a recognized nonprofit organization. Chapter eight explains how to find one, and how to avoid people who are not allowed to give legal help.

## Two appointments, two different times

The medical exam and the naturalization interview usually happen years apart:

- **The immigration medical exam** is usually part of applying for a green card from inside the United States. People applying from abroad have a similar exam with a panel physician overseas, arranged through the embassy or consulate.
- **The naturalization interview** comes later, when a permanent resident applies to become a citizen.

You may be reading this for one appointment or the other. Each part of the guide stands on its own.

# The immigration medical exam: who, what and why

## Why there is a medical exam

People applying for a green card from inside the United States usually need a medical exam. Its purpose is public health: to check for certain health conditions and to confirm that required vaccinations are up to date. It is not a general checkup, and it is not about whether you are healthy enough to work.

## Who does it: the civil surgeon

The exam must be done by a civil surgeon: a doctor designated by the immigration authorities to perform these exams. Your own family doctor cannot do it unless they are a designated civil surgeon.

Civil surgeons are usually private doctors or clinics. They set their own fees, and the exam is usually not covered by health insurance. Prices vary, so it is reasonable to call a few and ask.

## Finding a civil surgeon

The official immigration website has a search tool to find designated civil surgeons near you. Use it, and make sure the doctor you book is on that official list. Questions to ask when you call:

- What is the total fee, and what does it include?
- Are blood tests and vaccinations included, or extra?
- How many visits will it take?
- What should I bring?
- What languages do you speak?
- How long until the form is ready?

Write the answers in the civil surgeon comparison page at the back of this guide.

## What the exam includes

The exact steps depend on your age and history, but an exam usually includes:

- **A review of your medical history**, including any past illnesses, hospital stays and treatment.
- **A physical exam.**
- **A mental health screening**, which is usually a few questions.
- **A screening for substance use**, also usually questions.
- **Tests for certain infectious diseases**, often including a blood test for tuberculosis and, for some ages, blood or urine tests for certain other infections.
- **A review of your vaccination records**, and any vaccines you still need.

If a test result needs follow-up, the civil surgeon will explain what happens next. Many conditions can be treated, and having one does not automatically end an application. Ask the civil surgeon, and ask an immigration attorney if you are worried.

# Getting ready for the medical exam

## Vaccination records

Vaccinations are where many people need extra time. Before your appointment:

- Gather every vaccination record you have: childhood records from your home country, school records, records from any doctor in the United States, travel clinic cards.
- If records are in another language, bring them anyway and ask the civil surgeon whether a translation is needed.
- If you have no records, tell the civil surgeon. In some cases a blood test can show immunity, or the vaccine can be given.

The civil surgeon will tell you which vaccines are required for your age and which ones you still need. Some vaccines may need more than one dose over several weeks, so start early.

## What to bring

- Your government-issued photo identification, such as a passport.
- The medical exam form, if the civil surgeon asks you to bring a printed copy. Ask whether you should fill in your part beforehand, and do not sign it until the civil surgeon tells you to.
- Your vaccination records.
- A list of your current medicines.
- Records of any past or current treatment for tuberculosis or other conditions the civil surgeon asks about.
- Your glasses or contact lenses, if you wear them.
- Payment for the fee.

Use the medical exam packing list at the back of this guide.

## On the day

- Arrive a little early.
- Answer questions honestly. Do not hide past illnesses or treatment; it can cause far bigger problems later than the condition itself.
- Ask questions if anything is unclear. You can ask for an interpreter if you need one.
- Ask when your results and the completed form will be ready.

## After the exam

When the exam and any tests are done, the civil surgeon completes and signs the medical exam form. Follow the civil surgeon's instructions exactly about how the form is delivered, and whether it comes to you in a sealed envelope. If it does, do not open or damage the envelope; an opened envelope may not be accepted.

Ask the civil surgeon for a copy of your vaccination record and test results for your own files. Then note the date in your appointments log, and keep the receipt.

## Timing

The completed medical form is usually only valid for a limited period, and rules on when it must be submitted have changed over time. Check the current instructions on the official immigration website before booking, so the exam is not done too early or too late for your application.

# The naturalization interview: what happens, in order

When you apply to become a citizen, you will usually be called for an interview at a local immigration field office. The interview has two jobs: to go over your application with you, and to give you the English and civics tests.

## Before the day

You will receive an interview notice by mail, and it usually also appears in your online immigration account. Read it carefully. It gives the date, time and address, and lists what to bring.

If you cannot attend, follow the instructions on the notice to reschedule as soon as possible. Missing an interview without rescheduling can cause serious problems with your application.

## Arriving and security

- Arrive early. Allow time for parking, finding the building and security.
- Expect airport-style security screening at the entrance. Leave anything that might not be allowed at home.
- Check in at the window or desk with your interview notice and identification.
- Wait to be called. Waits can be long; bring water and something quiet to do, if allowed.

## In the interview room

An immigration officer will call your name and take you to a small office. Usually, in order:

- The officer asks you to stand and raise your right hand, and you promise to tell the truth.
- The officer checks your identification.
- The officer goes through your application with you, question by question, and may ask you to explain or update answers.
- The officer gives the English test: reading, writing and speaking.
- The officer gives the civics test.
- The officer tells you the result, or explains what happens next, and gives you a written notice.

The whole interview often takes well under an hour.

## Going over your application

The officer will ask about the information in your application: your addresses, jobs, travel outside the United States, marriages, children, taxes, and the many yes-or-no questions about your history.

- Read your own copy of your application several times before the interview. Know what you wrote.
- If something has changed since you applied, such as a new address, job, marriage or trip, tell the officer and bring papers that show it.
- If you do not understand a question, say so and ask the officer to repeat or rephrase it.
- Answer honestly. If you are not sure, say so.
- If any part of your history worries you, such as an arrest, a citation, a long trip abroad, or unpaid taxes, speak to an immigration attorney or accredited representative before the interview.

# The English and civics tests

## The English test

The English test has three parts:

- **Speaking.** The officer judges your spoken English from the conversation during the interview.
- **Reading.** You read aloud a short sentence in English.
- **Writing.** You write a short sentence in English that the officer reads to you.

The official immigration website publishes study materials, including vocabulary lists for the reading and writing tests. Most sentences use words from those lists.

## The civics test

The civics test asks questions about United States history and government, taken from an official list of questions and answers. The officer asks some of them out loud, and you answer out loud. You must answer a set number correctly to pass.

The civics test has been updated over time, and which version you take can depend on when you filed your application. Check the official study materials for the version that applies to you, and study from that list.

## Exceptions

Some applicants qualify for exceptions:

- Some older applicants who have been permanent residents for many years can take the civics test in their own language with an interpreter, and do not take the English test.
- Some applicants with a physical or developmental disability or a mental impairment that prevents them from learning English or civics can ask for an exception, using a medical certification form completed by a licensed medical professional.

The rules for each exception are specific. Check the official website or ask an accredited representative whether you qualify.

## If you do not pass

If you do not pass the English or civics test the first time, you are usually given a second chance at a later appointment, retaking the part you did not pass. Keep studying, and use your practice log.

# A study plan for the tests

A little practice every day works far better than a lot the night before.

## Several weeks out

- Download the official civics questions and answers for your version of the test.
- Download the official reading and writing vocabulary lists.
- Read through all the civics questions once, just to see them.
- Start a practice log at the back of this guide.

## Each day

- Practise a small group of civics questions until you can answer them without looking.
- Review the ones you got wrong on earlier days.
- Read a few vocabulary sentences aloud, and write a few from dictation; a friend or family member can read them to you.
- Speak English out loud for a few minutes, about your day, your family or your work.

## Ways to practise

- **Flashcards.** Question on one side, answer on the other.
- **A study partner.** Take turns asking questions.
- **The official practice tools**, including videos and apps from the official immigration website.
- **Free citizenship classes.** Many libraries, adult schools, community colleges and nonprofits offer free classes.

## The week before

- Practise with someone asking you the questions in random order, the way the officer will.
- Practise the interview itself: have someone ask you questions from your application.
- Prepare your papers using the checklist at the back of this guide.

# What to bring, and what comes after

## What to bring to the interview

Your interview notice lists what to bring. Common items include:

- your interview notice;
- your permanent resident card;
- a state-issued identification card or driver's license;
- all passports and travel documents you have used since becoming a permanent resident;
- papers that show changes or answers in your application, for example marriage certificates, divorce papers, birth certificates of children, tax records, papers about any arrests or court cases, or records showing child support was paid.

Use the interview packing checklist at the back of this guide, and bring originals and copies.

## The result

At the end of the interview, the officer usually gives you a written notice of the result. Broadly, it says one of three things:

- **Granted, or recommended for approval.** You passed and your application can be approved. You will be scheduled for, or sometimes take the same day, the oath ceremony.
- **Continued.** Something more is needed: for example, more papers, or a retest. The notice says what. Respond by the date given, and keep a copy of everything you send.
- **Denied.** The notice explains why. Speak to an immigration attorney or accredited representative about your options.

## The oath ceremony

You become a citizen when you take the Oath of Allegiance at a ceremony. You will receive a notice telling you when and where. At the ceremony you usually return your permanent resident card and receive your certificate of naturalization. Check the certificate for mistakes before you leave.

## After you become a citizen

- Keep your certificate somewhere safe. Read the warning printed on it about copying, and follow it; when an office needs to see it, ask what kind of copy they accept.
- Apply for a United States passport if you wish.
- Update your Social Security record.
- Register to vote.

# Where to get help, and how to avoid scams

## Free and low-cost help

- **Immigration attorneys.** Licensed lawyers who can advise on your case.
- **Accredited representatives.** Non-lawyers who work for nonprofit organizations recognized by the Department of Justice, and are allowed to give immigration legal help, often for free or a low fee. The Department of Justice publishes the official list of recognized organizations.
- **Citizenship classes.** Libraries, adult schools and nonprofits often run free classes for the tests.
- **The official immigration website and contact center.** For forms, fees, appointment information and case status.

## Warning signs of a scam

Some people take advantage of immigrants. Be careful if anyone:

- calls themselves a "notario" or "notary" and offers legal help; in the United States, a notary public is not a lawyer and may not give immigration legal advice;
- is not a licensed attorney or an accredited representative, but offers to choose your forms or tell you what to write;
- promises a faster result, special access, or a sure outcome;
- asks you to sign a blank form or a form with false information;
- keeps your original papers;
- asks for payment by gift card or wire transfer;
- claims to work for, or have a special connection to, the immigration agency.

Official forms are available free on the official immigration website. Never pay someone for a blank government form.

If you are unsure about someone, check whether they are a licensed attorney with the state bar, or on the official list of accredited representatives, before you pay or sign anything.
