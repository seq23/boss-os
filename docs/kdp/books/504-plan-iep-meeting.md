# 504 plan or IEP: the difference in plain words

If your child struggles at school because of a disability, a health condition or a learning difference, you will probably hear two names: a 504 plan and an IEP. Both are written plans a public school makes to help a student. They come from different laws, and they do different things.

## The IEP

IEP stands for Individualized Education Program. It comes from the federal special education law. An IEP is for a child who has a disability that fits one of the categories in that law and who needs specially designed instruction, often called special education, to make progress in school.

An IEP usually includes:

- where your child is now, in school skills and daily life;
- yearly goals;
- the special education and related services the school will provide, such as speech therapy, occupational therapy or counseling;
- accommodations and changes to how your child is taught or tested;
- how progress will be measured and reported to you.

An IEP is written by a team that includes you, the parent.

## The 504 plan

A 504 plan comes from a civil rights law that protects people with disabilities from discrimination in programs that receive federal money, which includes public schools. It is for a student whose disability substantially limits a major life activity, such as learning, reading, concentrating, breathing or walking, and who needs accommodations to have equal access to school.

A 504 plan usually lists accommodations: extra time on tests, a seat near the teacher, breaks, access to a nurse, a copy of class notes, use of a computer for writing. It usually does not change what your child is taught.

## Which one?

Very roughly: a child who needs specialized teaching usually needs an IEP; a child who can learn the regular curriculum with changes to how and where usually has a 504 plan. Some children start with one and move to the other.

This book does not tell you which plan your child should have or what services they need. That is decided by the school team, with you as a member, and the rules in your state. What this book does is help you keep your own record of the process, so you can take part fully.

## Why parents keep their own records

Schools keep files, but those files are written from the school's point of view, and staff change every year. Your own binder holds what you asked for, what was agreed, what was refused and why, and whether the plan is actually happening in the classroom. It means you never have to rely on memory, and you never walk into a meeting with less information than everyone else in the room.

# Setting up the binder in one afternoon

You need a sturdy binder, a hole punch, a pack of dividers and a pen. If you prefer, you can scan everything into folders on your phone instead; the order is the same.

## The sections

Use one divider for each section, in this order:

- **Contacts.** Names, roles and email addresses for the teacher, case manager, school psychologist, therapists, principal and the district's special education office.
- **Current plan.** The current IEP or 504 plan, the whole thing.
- **Evaluations.** Every evaluation and test report, from the school and from outside providers.
- **Progress.** Progress reports, report cards and state test results.
- **Meetings.** Your meeting notes and any meeting summaries the school sends.
- **Requests.** Copies of every written request you make and every reply.
- **School messages.** Printouts or an index of emails and notes from school.
- **Health and outside reports.** Letters from doctors, therapists or tutors.
- **Discipline and attendance.** Any discipline notices and attendance records.
- **Samples of work.** A few pieces of your child's work from each term, dated.

## Newest on top

In each section, put the newest paper on top. Write the date you received it in the top corner if it is not obvious.

## One index page

At the front of the binder, keep a one-page index: each section, and the papers in it with their dates. It takes a few minutes to update and saves a great deal of time in a meeting.

## Copies, not originals

Keep originals at home. Bring copies to meetings, and give copies, never originals, to anyone who asks.

## Getting the papers you are missing

Parents can usually ask to see and get copies of their child's school records. Ask the school in writing. Log the request and the date you receive the records.

# Asking in writing

Requests made in a hallway or a phone call are easy to forget, and there is no record of when they were made. A short written request is clearer for everyone, and in many cases the date of a written request matters, because timelines for evaluations and responses often start from it.

## What to put in writing

- A request for an evaluation for special education or a 504 plan.
- A request for a meeting.
- A request for copies of your child's records.
- A request to change something in the plan.
- A concern that the plan is not being followed.

## Keep it short

An invented example of an evaluation request:

> Dear principal and case manager,
>
> I am writing to request an evaluation of my son for special education services and for a 504 plan. He is struggling to read and write at grade level, and his teacher has told me he often cannot finish written work. I give my consent for the school to evaluate him and would like to know what happens next and when.
>
> Thank you.

An invented example of a concern:

> Dear Ms. Teacher,
>
> My daughter's 504 plan includes extra time on tests. She told me she did not get extra time on Friday's math test. Could you let me know whether the extra time was given, and how we can make sure it is given going forward?
>
> Thank you.

## Send it in a way you can find again

Email is usually easiest, because it is dated. Keep a copy in the requests section of your binder, and log it in the requests log: the date, who you sent it to, what you asked for, and later the reply and its date.

## If there is no reply

Wait a reasonable time, then send a short follow-up that refers to your first request by date. If there is still no reply, write to the next person up, such as the principal or the district's special education office, and include a copy of your earlier messages.

# Before the meeting

Most parents find IEP and 504 meetings intimidating. There may be six or more school staff around the table, using terms you do not know, with a draft already written. Preparation makes a large difference.

## Ask for the papers in advance

Ask, in writing, for copies of any draft plan, evaluation reports and progress data a few days before the meeting, so you have time to read them. Not every school will send a draft, but it is reasonable to ask.

## Read and mark up

Read the draft or the current plan. Mark anything you do not understand, anything that does not match what you see at home, and anything missing. Compare the goals with the progress reports: is your child moving toward them?

## Write a one-page parent summary

Many parents bring a single page to hand out. It might include:

- a few lines about your child: strengths, interests, what motivates them;
- what you see at home, such as homework taking hours, worry before school, or new skills;
- your main concerns, in order;
- what you would like the team to consider.

Keep it to one page and bring enough copies for everyone.

## Prepare your questions

A list of questions keeps you from forgetting what matters in the moment. Some that parents often find useful:

- What does my child do well? What is hardest for them right now?
- How is progress on each goal measured, and how often will I hear about it?
- Who is responsible for each service and accommodation, and how often does it happen?
- What happens if an accommodation is not working?
- What does this term or abbreviation mean?
- Can you show me the data behind that?
- What would it look like if this goal were met?
- How will this be handled on days with a substitute teacher?
- What is the next step if we do not agree today?

## Who to bring

You can usually bring someone with you: your child's other parent, a relative, a friend for support, an advocate, or an outside professional who knows your child. Let the school know in advance who is coming. For older children, ask whether your child should attend part of the meeting; their voice matters.

# During the meeting

## Take notes, or bring a note-taker

It is hard to listen, think and write at the same time. If you can, bring someone whose only job is to take notes. Use the meeting notes log: who attended, what was proposed, what was agreed, what was refused and the reason given, and who will follow up on what, by when.

Some parents ask to record the meeting. Rules about recording differ by state and district, and some require advance notice. Ask the school beforehand rather than recording without telling anyone.

## Ask for clarity

If you do not understand a term, ask. If a statement is vague, such as "we'll check in on that", ask who will check in, how and when. If something is said about your child that does not match what you see, say so calmly and give an example.

## Ask to see it in writing

When the team agrees to something, ask how it will be written into the plan. When the team says no to a request, you can ask for the reason, and in many cases you can ask for that decision in writing.

## Take a break when you need one

You can ask for a short break, or to finish the meeting another day, if you need time to think or to read something new. You do not have to decide everything in one sitting.

## You do not have to sign on the spot

Depending on your state and the type of plan, a signature might mean you attended, or that you agree. Ask what your signature means before you sign. You can usually take the plan home to read before agreeing. If you agree with part of it and not all, ask how to say so.

# After the meeting and through the year

## Send a recap

The same day or the next, send a short email to the case manager summing up the meeting: what was agreed, what was refused, and who will do what by when. Ask them to let you know if anything in your summary is wrong. File a copy.

An invented example:

> Thank you for meeting today. To confirm what I understood: the team agreed to add a daily check-in with the counselor and to start speech therapy twice a week. The request for a one-to-one aide was not agreed; the reason given was that data from this term does not show the need. Mr. Case Manager will send the updated plan by the end of next week. Please let me know if I have misunderstood anything.

## Check the plan is followed

A plan only helps if it happens. Each week, take a minute to fill in a line of the weekly check log: an accommodation or service, whether it was given as far as you know, what your child said about it, and what you noticed. Over a term, these lines show clearly whether the plan is working.

## Keep talking to your child

Ask open questions: "Did you get your breaks today?" "How did the test feel?" Children often notice when an accommodation is missing before anyone else does.

## When to ask for another meeting

You do not have to wait for the yearly review. You can ask for a meeting in writing when:

- your child is not making progress;
- an accommodation or service is not being provided;
- something new has happened, such as a new diagnosis, a move, or a change at home;
- discipline problems are increasing;
- you have new information from an outside evaluation.

## Transition times

Moves to a new school, to middle school or high school, and planning for life after school are big moments. Ask early how the plan will follow your child, and keep copies of everything from the old school.

# Where to get help

## Free help for parents

Every state has at least one federally funded Parent Training and Information Center. These centers help families of children with disabilities understand their rights, prepare for meetings and resolve disagreements, at no cost. The official sources at the back of this book list the center for your state.

Other places to turn:

- **Your state's protection and advocacy agency**, which helps people with disabilities with rights issues.
- **Local parent support groups**, often run by parents who have been through the same process.
- **Disability-specific organizations** for conditions such as autism, dyslexia, ADHD or hearing loss.
- **Advocates and special education lawyers**, some of whom offer free or low-cost help.

## When you disagree

Disagreements happen. Most are settled by talking: another meeting, a phone call with the case manager, a meeting with the principal or the district's special education office. If that does not work, the law gives parents formal ways to resolve disputes, such as mediation and complaints. The official sources at the back of this book list your state's options. Your parent center can explain them.

## Look after yourself

Advocating for a child is a long road. Find other parents who understand. Take breaks. You know your child better than anyone at the table, and that knowledge is worth bringing, calmly, year after year.
