# Is a courthouse wedding right for you?

A courthouse wedding, also called a city hall wedding or a civil ceremony, is a legal marriage performed by a judge, a magistrate, a clerk or another official, usually in a government building. It can be over in ten minutes. It can also be the heart of a day you remember for the rest of your life.

Couples choose it for many reasons:

- **Cost.** A courthouse ceremony costs a small fraction of a traditional wedding.
- **Speed.** It can be planned in weeks, sometimes days.
- **Simplicity.** No venue, no caterer, no seating chart, no months of decisions.
- **Privacy.** Just the two of you, or a handful of the people who matter most.
- **Practical reasons.** Health insurance, immigration paperwork, a move, a deployment, or simply wanting to be married now and celebrate later.

## The honest trade-offs

A courthouse wedding is simpler, but it is not for everyone. Think through these before you book:

- **Limited guests.** Many courtrooms and ceremony rooms hold only a small group. Some limit guests to a handful.
- **Limited control.** The officiant, the room, the words and the timing are largely set by the court.
- **Hurt feelings.** Some relatives may be disappointed not to be invited, or not to see a big wedding.
- **Less ceremony.** If you have dreamed of walking down an aisle, music, readings and a big party, you may want those things too. Many couples have a courthouse ceremony and a party later.

None of these is a reason not to do it. They are reasons to plan on purpose.

# The marriage license, step by step

The license is the legal heart of a courthouse wedding. Get it right and everything else is easy.

## Where to get it

In most of the United States, marriage licenses are issued by a county office, often called the county clerk, the clerk of court, the recorder or the probate court. You usually apply in the county where the ceremony will take place, though some states let you use a license from any county in the state.

Start by finding your county clerk's marriage license page. Most list everything you need. If anything is unclear, call.

## Questions to ask your county clerk

Use this list. Write the answers in the license worksheet at the back of this planner.

- Do we both need to apply in person, together?
- Do we need an appointment, or can we walk in? Can we start the application online?
- What identification do we each need? Is a driver's license enough, or do we also need a birth certificate?
- Do we need our social security numbers?
- If either of us was married before, what papers do we need, such as a divorce decree or a death certificate, and how recent must it be?
- What is the fee, and what forms of payment do you take?
- Is there a waiting period between getting the license and holding the ceremony?
- How long is the license valid? When does it expire?
- Do we need witnesses at the ceremony? How many, and do they need identification?
- After the ceremony, who returns the signed license, and how do we order certified copies of the marriage certificate?

## Waiting periods and expiry

Some states make you wait a short time between getting the license and marrying. Others do not. Licenses also expire after a set period. Plan your ceremony date between those two points, and write both dates in your countdown.

## Name changes

If one or both of you plan to change your name, ask the clerk how your state handles it. In many places the name you will use after marriage is recorded on the license application. Afterwards, the certified marriage certificate is usually what you show to change your name with Social Security, the motor vehicle office, your bank and your employer.

## Certified copies

Order a few certified copies of your marriage certificate once it is recorded. You may need them for name changes, insurance, benefits and immigration papers.

# Booking the ceremony

## Who can marry you

Many courthouses offer civil ceremonies performed by a judge, magistrate, justice of the peace or clerk. Some courthouses no longer offer ceremonies at all, and refer couples to an officiant elsewhere. Others hold ceremonies only on certain days. Ask your county clerk who performs ceremonies and how to book.

If your courthouse does not offer ceremonies, you can still have a small civil wedding: get the license at the courthouse, then marry with an authorized officiant in a park, a restaurant's private room or your living room.

## Booking

- Book early if you want a particular date, especially popular ones like Valentine's Day, the end of the year, or a date with repeating numbers.
- Ask what time to arrive, and how long the ceremony slot is.
- Ask whether there is a fee for the ceremony, separate from the license.
- Ask whether you can bring your own vows, rings, music or readings.
- Ask how many guests the room holds and whether photos are allowed inside.

## What happens in the room

Every courthouse is different, but a typical civil ceremony goes something like this:

- You check in at a desk and show your license and identification.
- You wait, sometimes with other couples, until you are called.
- The officiant greets you and may ask a few questions to confirm details.
- The ceremony itself is short: a few words from the officiant, your agreement to marry, the exchange of rings if you have them, and the declaration that you are married.
- You, your witnesses and the officiant sign the license.
- You kiss, if you like, and step out married.

Ask beforehand whether you can say your own vows. Some officiants are happy to make room for them; others use a set script.

## Witnesses

Many states require one or two adult witnesses. Some courthouses can provide a witness if you have none; ask. If you bring witnesses, make sure they bring identification if needed, and arrive on time.

# The small guest list and telling people

## Decide who comes

Start with the room's limit, then decide. Common choices:

- **Just the two of you**, plus any required witnesses.
- **Immediate family only.**
- **A tiny group**: parents, siblings, children, and one or two close friends.

Whatever you choose, apply the rule the same way to everyone. "Immediate family only" is easier to explain than a list with exceptions.

## Telling people who are not invited

Tell close family before the wedding if you think they will be hurt to hear afterwards. Keep it warm and simple. An example:

> We wanted to tell you ourselves: we're getting married at the courthouse next month, with just our parents there. It's what feels right for us. We'd love to celebrate with you at a party this summer.

You do not owe anyone a long explanation.

## Announcing it afterwards

After the wedding, many couples share a photo and a short message, or send printed announcements. If you plan a party later, the announcement can include the date.

## When feelings are hurt

Someone may be upset. Listen, thank them for caring, and repeat your decision kindly. An invitation to a later celebration often helps.

# Outfits, flowers, rings and photos

## What to wear

There is no dress code for a courthouse wedding. Some couples wear a full wedding gown and suit; others wear a favorite dress, a jumpsuit, or a jacket and jeans. A few practical points:

- Courthouses have security screening. Leave anything that might be a problem at home, and allow time to go through.
- Courtroom floors can be hard and steps can be steep. Choose shoes you can walk in.
- Bring a layer. Government buildings can be cold.

## Flowers

A small bouquet and a boutonniere photograph beautifully against plain courthouse walls. A local florist can make a simple bouquet, or you can buy flowers from a market and tie them with ribbon the morning of the wedding.

## Rings

Rings are not required for a legal marriage. If you plan to exchange them, decide who will carry them, usually a witness or one of you, in a pocket that zips.

## Photos

Photos are where a courthouse wedding often shines: columns, grand stairways, old wooden doors, city streets.

- Ask the courthouse whether photos are allowed inside the ceremony room.
- Consider hiring a photographer for an hour. Many offer short courthouse sessions. Check reviews and look at full galleries, not just highlights.
- Or ask a friend with a good camera or phone to take photos. Give them a short list of must-have shots.
- Walk outside afterwards for portraits on the steps or nearby streets.

The photo list at the back of this planner gives you a starting point.

# The celebration afterwards

The ceremony may be short, but the day does not have to be.

## Ideas

- **A long lunch** at a favorite restaurant with your guests.
- **Dinner for two** somewhere special.
- **A picnic** in a park near the courthouse.
- **Drinks and dessert** at home with close friends.
- **A party later**, weeks or months afterwards, for everyone you could not invite to the ceremony.

## Booking a meal

If you plan a meal right after the ceremony, book a table for a time that allows for delays at the courthouse. Tell the restaurant it is a wedding; many will add a small touch.

## A later party

A later celebration can be as simple or as big as you like: a backyard barbecue, a dinner, a dance party. Because the legal part is done, there is no pressure. Some couples renew their vows or read a short message to guests at the party.

# Small touches that make it yours

A courthouse ceremony can feel brief and official. A few small touches turn it into a day that feels like yours, without adding much cost or planning.

## Before the ceremony

- **Get ready together, or apart.** Some couples like to see each other first at the courthouse steps; others get ready side by side. Decide on purpose.
- **A first look.** If you get ready apart, meet somewhere near the courthouse ten minutes early, just the two of you, before anyone else arrives.
- **A letter.** Write each other a short letter to read on the morning of the wedding.
- **Something borrowed.** A grandparent's handkerchief, a parent's cufflinks, a friend's hair pin.

## During the ceremony

- **Your own vows.** Even two or three sentences, if the officiant allows them. See the next chapter.
- **A reading.** A short poem or passage read by a guest, if there is time.
- **Music.** Some rooms allow a phone to play a song as you walk in or out. Ask first.
- **Hands.** Hold hands. Look at each other, not the officiant.

## After the ceremony

- **The exit.** Walk out to applause from your guests. Some couples ask guests to wave ribbons or blow bubbles on the steps outside; check that the courthouse allows it.
- **The first photo as a married couple.** Decide where it will be taken before the day.
- **A small cake.** A single cake, or cupcakes, at lunch or at home.
- **A toast.** Ask one guest to say a few words over a meal.
- **A keepsake.** Frame a copy of your marriage certificate with a photo from the day, or keep the license envelope with your vows in a box.

## For guests who cannot be there

- **A video call** during the ceremony, if the room allows it and someone can hold the phone.
- **A short video** recorded after the ceremony, sent the same day.
- **A shared album** where guests at the later party can add their photos.

# Short vows for a courthouse ceremony

Civil ceremonies are short, so short vows fit them well. If your officiant allows personal vows, three to five sentences each is plenty. A simple shape:

- something you love about your partner;
- something you promise;
- something about your life together.

Three invented examples, to show the shape. Write your own words, not these.

> I love how you make every ordinary day feel like it matters. I promise to listen, even when I'm tired, and to make you laugh when you need it. Wherever we go next, I want to go there with you.

> You are the calmest person I know, and the bravest. I promise to be honest with you, to stand next to you when things are hard, and to dance with you in the kitchen. I choose you today and every day after.

> When I met you, I stopped looking. I promise to keep choosing us, to say sorry first, and to always save you the last bite. I can't wait to grow old with you.

Print your vows on a small card. Practise them out loud once or twice before the day. If you cry, pause, breathe, and carry on. Everyone in the room is on your side.

# Your countdown checklist

Work through these in order. Adjust the timing to your county's rules on waiting periods and license expiry.

- [ ] Find your county clerk's marriage license page

- [ ] Find out whether your courthouse performs ceremonies, and how to book
- [ ] Pick a date and book the ceremony
- [ ] Decide on your guest list
- [ ] Tell close family who will not be invited

## A few weeks before

- [ ] Gather identification and any papers the clerk asked for
- [ ] Book a photographer, or ask a friend
- [ ] Choose outfits and shoes
- [ ] Order or plan flowers
- [ ] Book a table for a meal afterwards
- [ ] Confirm your witnesses

## When the timing is right for your county

- [ ] Apply for the marriage license together
- [ ] Check the license for spelling mistakes before you leave the counter
- [ ] Note the license expiry date

## The week of the wedding

- [ ] Confirm the ceremony time and where to check in
- [ ] Send guests the address, the arrival time and a note about security screening
- [ ] Print your vows if you are saying your own
- [ ] Pack the day-of bag

## After the wedding

- [ ] Make sure the signed license is returned to the clerk
- [ ] Order certified copies of the marriage certificate
- [ ] Start any name changes
- [ ] Update insurance, benefits and emergency contacts
- [ ] Send announcements or thank-you notes
