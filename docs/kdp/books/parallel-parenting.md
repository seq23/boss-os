# What parallel parenting is, and when it helps

Most advice about raising children after a separation assumes two parents who can sit at the same table, talk through a problem and agree. That is co-parenting, and when it works it is a gift to a child.

Sometimes it does not work. Every message turns into an argument. A handoff in a parking lot becomes a scene your child watches from the back seat. You dread your phone lighting up. You have tried being reasonable, being firm, being patient, and none of it has changed the pattern.

Parallel parenting is the quieter alternative. Instead of trying to parent together, each parent runs their own time with the child, and contact between the parents shrinks to the few things that truly need it: the schedule, the handoffs, school, health and money for the child. Everything else is left alone.

## The idea in one picture

Picture two train tracks running side by side. Both lead to the same place, a child who is loved and looked after. The tracks do not cross. Each parent drives their own train on their own track, by their own rules at their own house, and the only place the tracks meet is the station platform where the child changes trains.

That platform, the handoff, and the few messages that arrange it are where this book spends most of its time.

## What it is not

Parallel parenting is not giving up on your child's other parent as a parent. Your child still has two homes and two people who love them.

It is not cutting the other parent out of information they need. School events, illness, a change in a doctor's advice: these still get shared, in writing, briefly.

It is not a punishment or a strategy to make the other parent look bad. The point is to lower the temperature, for your child's sake and for yours.

It is also not a legal term with one fixed meaning. Some parenting plans and custody orders use the phrase and spell out rules for it; many do not. If you have a written plan or order, it comes first. Nothing in this book overrides it, and if anything here seems to conflict with yours, follow your plan and ask the court's self-help center or a lawyer what applies where you live.

## When it tends to help

Parents usually reach for parallel parenting when one or more of these is true:

- Almost every conversation about the child becomes an argument about something else.
- Handoffs are tense, loud or late, and the child is visibly upset by them.
- One parent sends long messages, many messages, or messages at all hours, and the other feels worn down by them.
- Agreements made in person are remembered differently later.
- A counselor, mediator or parenting coordinator has suggested less contact between the parents.

If there is violence, threats, stalking or any danger to you or your child, parallel parenting is not enough on its own. Call 911 in an emergency. Protective orders and safety planning exist for exactly this, and the official sources at the back of this book list where to start in your state. A local domestic violence program can help you plan, confidentially.

## The scope rule for this whole book

This book asks you to keep a record: dated notes, copies of messages, receipts, school papers. That record has limits, and they matter.

Keep only what you already have, what was sent to you, and what you wrote yourself. Never go into the other parent's phone, email or accounts. Never install anything on a device that is not yours. Never record anyone in secret. Never coach your child to report on the other home. Rules about recording and privacy differ from state to state, and breaking them can hurt you and your child far more than any note could help. If you are unsure whether something is allowed, ask a lawyer before you do it.

A record you keep is your own account of events, made at the time. It is not proof of anything on its own, and nothing in this book can say how a judge, mediator or agency will see it. What it can do is help the people you turn to for help understand what has been happening, quickly and calmly.

# The written rules: one channel, short messages, set times

In a high-conflict separation, the channel is half the problem. Texts at midnight, a phone call during dinner, a voicemail, an email, a comment passed through a grandparent, a note in the child's backpack. Each one is a small spark, and there are dozens a week.

Parallel parenting starts by narrowing all of it to one channel, used one way.

## Pick one channel

Choose a single place for all messages about your child. Many parents use a co-parenting app because it keeps every message with a date and time and is hard to edit later. Others use one email address kept only for this. Plain text messages can work too, though they mix with everything else on your phone.

What matters is that it is one place, used every time, and that it keeps a dated copy you can look back on. If your parenting plan or a court order names a channel, use that one.

Tell the other parent once, in writing, which channel you will use:

> From now on I will answer messages about our child in this app. I will check it each morning and each evening. For an emergency involving our child, call me.

Then stick to it. If a message arrives somewhere else, you can answer in the agreed channel: "I saw your text. Answering here so it stays in one place."

## Answer only the logistics

Most messages in a high-conflict separation contain two things: a question about the child, and something else. The something else might be blame, an old argument, a comment about your new partner, a remark about your parenting.

Answer the question. Leave the something else alone. Every reply you write to the something else keeps that fire going.

A message like this:

> You're late again, like always, which shows how much you care. Is she going to the party Saturday or are you going to ruin that too?

has one question in it: is the child going to the party Saturday. The reply is:

> Yes, she's going to the party Saturday. I'll drop her off and pick her up.

Nothing else. It can feel unfair to let an insult stand. It is not agreement. It is a decision not to spend your energy, or your child's peace, on it.

## Set response times

You do not owe anyone an instant reply. Decide when you will check the channel, for example once in the morning and once in the evening, and tell the other parent. Then answer at those times.

Urgent matters about your child are different: an injury, a hospital visit, a missed pickup. Agree that these get a phone call, and only these.

Response times protect your evenings and your sleep. They also mean you answer when you are calm, not in the minute after an upsetting message lands.

## The BIFF shape

A widely used shape for these replies is called BIFF: Brief, Informative, Friendly, Firm.

- **Brief.** A few sentences. Long messages invite long replies.
- **Informative.** The facts needed to answer the question or make the plan. No opinions, no history.
- **Friendly.** One polite line. "Thanks for letting me know." "Hope the trip goes well." It is not for the other parent's sake as much as for anyone who may read the messages later, including your child someday.
- **Firm.** A clear end. A yes, a no, a time, a place. No open questions that invite another round.

An invented example. The other parent writes:

> I can't believe you signed him up for soccer without asking me. You always do this. I'm not driving him to any practices on my weekends and you can't make me.

A BIFF reply:

> Thanks for telling me. Practices are on weekday evenings, during my time, so I'll take him. The two weekend games this season fall on my weekends too. I'll send the schedule here when the coach sends it.

It answers the real worry (the weekend driving), gives the facts, stays polite, and ends.

Use the reply-draft log at the back of this book for the hard ones. Write the draft there first, sleep on it if you can, cut it in half, then send.

## Before you press send

Ask yourself four questions:

- Would I be comfortable if a judge, a mediator, or my child as an adult read this?
- Does it answer the question that was asked, and only that?
- Is there any sarcasm, any "always" or "never", any comment about the other parent as a person?
- Is it shorter than the message I received?

If a reply fails any of these, edit it before it goes.

## What not to put in writing

- Threats of any kind, even joking ones.
- Comments on the other parent's new partner, family or home.
- Diagnoses. Do not call the other parent a narcissist or any other label in a message, even if you believe it. It turns a logistics message into a fight and it is not your role.
- Your child's private words about the other parent.
- Anything you would not say in front of your child.

# Handoffs without a scene

The handoff is the one place in parallel parenting where both parents are in the same spot at the same time. It is also where children most often see conflict. A calm handoff is one of the kindest things you can give your child.

## Choose the place

Some families hand off at school or daycare, so the parents never meet: one parent drops the child off in the morning, the other picks up in the afternoon. If your schedule allows it, this is often the calmest option of all.

Otherwise, choose a neutral, public, well-lit place. A busy store entrance, a library, a coffee shop. Some police and sheriff's departments set aside a spot in their parking lot for exchanges; call the non-emergency line to ask. If your plan or order names a place, use that one.

Avoid each other's homes if handoffs there have gone badly. Doorsteps invite people inside, and arguments follow.

## Plan the minute

Most handoff trouble happens in the gap: waiting, chatting, a question asked across a car roof. Plan the minute so there is no gap.

- Be on time. If you will be late, send one short message in the agreed channel as soon as you know.
- Have the child's things ready in one bag: clothes, medicine, homework, the favorite toy.
- Stay in or near your car unless your child needs help.
- Say a warm goodbye to your child. Keep it short and cheerful even if you feel otherwise.
- Do not discuss schedules, money or problems at the handoff. "Let's put that in the app" is a complete sentence.
- Leave.

## If it starts to go wrong

If the other parent starts an argument, do not answer it. Say one calm line and step away: "I'll answer in the app tonight." Then focus on your child.

If you feel unsafe, leave with your child if that is permitted and safe, or stay in a locked car and call 911. Then write down what happened as soon as you can.

## Write it down after

After each handoff, take two minutes to fill in a line of the handoff log: the date, the planned time and place, the actual time, who did the handoff, and how it went, in plain facts.

Most entries will be boring. "On time. Calm. She had her inhaler." That is the point. A long run of boring entries is a record of a calm, workable arrangement, and it also makes the rare difficult entry easy to see.

## Things that travel between homes

Clothes, medicine, school projects and sports gear cause many small disputes. Some ideas that help:

- Keep a full set of everyday clothes and toiletries at each home, so very little has to travel.
- Medicines travel in a labeled pouch with the dose written on a card, and the log notes that they went.
- School items travel in the backpack, and the backpack is checked by each parent before the handoff.
- A note in the bag, short and factual, can replace a conversation: "Fever yesterday, fine today. Last dose of medicine at seven this morning."

# Keeping the record: dated, factual, boring

You may already have a pile of screenshots and a head full of memories. A record is something different: entries made on the day, in one place, written so that a stranger could read them and understand what happened.

The people who may help you, such as a lawyer, a mediator, a parenting coordinator or a court self-help center, are busy. They can work with a clear record. They struggle with a box of loose papers and a long, angry story.

## What makes an entry useful

- **The same day.** Memory changes quickly, especially under stress. An entry written that evening is far more useful than one written a month later.
- **Date and time first.** Every entry starts with them.
- **What was planned, then what happened.** "Pickup planned for six at the library. Other parent arrived at seven fifteen. No message before."
- **Facts, not feelings or guesses.** Write what you saw and heard. Leave out what you think it means and why you think the other parent did it.
- **Exact words only when you heard them yourself.** Put them in quotation marks. If you did not hear them, do not quote them.
- **Short.** A few lines.
- **Corrections as new lines.** If you made a mistake, do not erase or rewrite. Add a new dated line: "Correction to the entry above: the time was six thirty, not six."

## A weak entry and a useful one

An invented weak entry:

> He was late AGAIN like always and didn't even care. Obviously trying to mess up her schedule because he's jealous. She was crying all night because of him.

An invented useful entry about the same evening:

> Tuesday. Pickup planned for six at the library. Other parent arrived at seven fifteen. No message before. Our daughter waited inside with me. She asked twice if he was coming. She cried at bedtime and said she was tired.

The second one is calmer and easier to read, and it lets the reader draw their own conclusions. Notice that it records what the child said and did, not what you believe caused it.

## Keep it in one place

Choose one place for the log: this book, the free printable logs on the website, a notebook kept only for this, or an app that dates each entry. Do not spread it across sticky notes, texts to yourself and memory.

## Messages you receive

Messages sent to you are part of your record. Keep them where they arrived, and do not delete them. If you take screenshots, make sure the date and the sender are visible, and capture the whole conversation rather than a single line out of context. Keep a backup somewhere safe, such as a second device or a folder you control.

Never edit or crop a copy in a way that changes what it says. Never alter a date.

The messages log at the back of this book is not for copying every message. It is an index, so you can find a message later: the date, who sent it, how, a few words about what it was about, and whether it needed a reply.

## Money for your child

Child expenses cause steady friction: who paid for the school shoes, the field trip, the doctor's co-payment. Keep receipts for what you pay, and log each one with the date, what it was for, the amount and who paid. If your plan or order splits certain costs, send the request for the other parent's share in the agreed channel, with a copy of the receipt, and log the answer.

## How long to keep things

Keep everything until your case is fully finished and for a good while after. Your lawyer or the court's self-help center can tell you more about your situation.

# Decisions, school and doctors: whose papers go where

In a parallel arrangement, day-to-day decisions belong to the parent who has the child that day. What your child eats, when bedtime is, which park you go to: these are yours on your time and the other parent's on theirs. You do not need to agree, and you do not need to discuss them.

Bigger decisions, usually school, health care, religion and activities that cross both homes, are usually covered by your parenting plan or order. Some plans give one parent the final say in an area. Some require both to agree. Some split areas between the parents. Read yours, and follow it.

## School

Most schools will give each parent their own access to the school's portal, emails and report cards, unless an order says otherwise. Ask the school office to set you up directly, so you do not have to rely on the other parent to forward anything.

- Ask to be on the school's email list yourself.
- Attend conferences separately if sitting together is tense. Many teachers will happily arrange two short meetings.
- Keep your own copies of report cards, notices and any plan the school makes for your child.
- If you learn something the other parent needs to know, such as a schedule change or a concern from a teacher, send it in the agreed channel in one or two sentences.

## Doctors, dentists and counselors

The same idea applies. Ask each provider's office to put both parents on the child's file, unless an order says otherwise, and to send appointment reminders to both.

- Share illnesses and medicine instructions in writing at the handoff or in the channel.
- If one parent takes the child to an appointment, send a short summary afterwards: what the doctor said, any new medicine, any follow-up.
- Keep your own copies of visit summaries and prescriptions.

## Activities

Activities that happen only on your time are yours to arrange. Activities that cross into the other parent's time need agreement, or the rules in your plan. When you propose one, put it in writing with the facts: what it is, when, how much, and who would drive on which days. Give the other parent time to answer. Log the request and the answer in the schedule-changes log.

## Schedule changes

Requests to swap days are a common source of conflict. Some ideas that help:

- Ask in writing, early, in one message with the exact dates.
- Offer the swap you are proposing in return.
- Accept a no without argument.
- When you agree to a change, confirm it in writing: "Confirming: I'll have her Friday the twelfth and you'll have her Friday the nineteenth."
- Log every request, by either parent, with the answer and what actually happened.

# Keeping your child out of the middle

Children of separated parents often say the hardest part was not the separation itself but being caught between their parents. Parallel parenting exists largely to protect them from that.

## No messages through your child

Never ask your child to carry a message, a check, a request or a complaint to the other parent. Even something that seems small, such as "Tell your dad you need new shoes", puts your child in the role of go-between. Use the channel.

## No questioning after visits

It is natural to want to know how the time at the other home went. But a string of questions about the other parent, their partner or their house turns your child into a reporter. Ask the open, warm questions you would ask after any outing: "What was your favorite part of the weekend?" Then listen, and let it go.

## When your child repeats something

Children sometimes repeat things said at the other home: "Dad says you don't want me to have fun." Stay calm. You do not need to defend yourself to your child or correct the other parent. Something like "I love you very much, and I love having fun with you" is enough. If it worries you, write a short, factual line in your log: the date, and your child's words exactly as they said them.

## Speak kindly, or not at all

Your child is half of each of you. Criticism of the other parent can land as criticism of them. If you cannot say something kind, say nothing.

## Signs to bring to a professional

Every child reacts to a separation differently. If you notice lasting changes, such as trouble sleeping, school problems, withdrawal, anger, or a child refusing to go to either home, talk to your child's doctor or a counselor who works with children. This book is not a substitute for that help.

# When to bring in help

Parallel parenting lowers conflict, but it does not solve everything. Sometimes the right next step is to bring in someone with training.

## People who can help

- **A parenting coordinator.** In some places, a court can appoint a parenting coordinator to help high-conflict parents carry out their plan and settle day-to-day disputes. Ask the court's self-help center whether this exists where you live.
- **A mediator.** A neutral person who helps parents reach agreements, for example on a new schedule.
- **A family counselor or a child's counselor.** For your child's well-being, and sometimes for co-parenting skills.
- **The court's self-help center or family law facilitator.** Most state court websites have one. They cannot be your lawyer, but they can explain forms and procedures.
- **Legal aid.** Free or low-cost legal help for people with lower incomes.
- **The local bar association's lawyer referral service.** A way to find a family lawyer, often with a low-cost first consultation.
- **A domestic violence program.** Confidential help with safety planning.

The official sources at the back of this book list court and agency pages for your state.

## Bringing your record

When you meet someone who is helping you, bring copies, never your only originals. Bring the checklist of papers below with the boxes ticked, your handoff and message logs, and a one-page summary of the main events in date order. Ask them what else would help.

## Look after yourself too

High-conflict co-parenting is exhausting. Sleep, friends, exercise, a counselor of your own, a support group for separated parents: these are not luxuries. A calmer you is one of the kindest things your child can have at either home.

If you or your child are ever in danger, call 911.

# Checklist: papers to keep in one place

Tick each group when you have a copy in your folder. Bring copies to meetings, never your only originals.

- [ ] Your current parenting plan or custody order
- [ ] Messages the other parent sent you, kept where they arrived and backed up
- [ ] Your handoff log
- [ ] School papers for each child: report cards, notices, any school plan
- [ ] Medical papers for each child: visit summaries, prescriptions, insurance cards
- [ ] Child expense receipts and a note of who paid
- [ ] Notes from any mediator, counselor or parenting coordinator
- [ ] Your own short calendar of what was planned and what happened

What matters differs by state and by family. Your lawyer, mediator or the court's self-help center can say which papers matter for yours.
